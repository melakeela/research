# HOLD-011 — The Meluhha interpreter inscription

**Opened:** 2026-09-09, domain R, from step 13.
**Blocks:** `DRM-027` (recorded `HOLD`); and, on MelaKeela itself, the claim
that carries the most weight on `meluhha-trade.html` (`IC-R-002`).
**Typed absence:** `DR-NEG-013`, `NOT PUBLISHED` in the subset searched — a
corpus-coverage absence, **not** an absence in the record.

## What is needed

The cuneiform inscription naming a professional **interpreter of the Meluhhan
language** — best known from a cylinder seal — with its museum number, a
published edition, a photograph or line drawing, and the transliteration of the
title as actually written.

## Why this one object matters more than its size suggests

On MelaKeela's `meluhha-trade.html` it carries a paragraph headed *"the detail
that matters most"*, and the conclusion drawn from it is a **language** claim:
that the Indus people spoke a distinct language of their own. That is the
single largest inferential step this platform makes from a materials-and-
corridors fact to a language fact, and it rests on one object.

Under the domain's own rule — material movement is not population movement is
not language movement — an object carrying that much weight has to be in hand.
It is not.

## What this unit did establish, and what it does not establish

Searching the CDLI transliteration corpus (`SRC-103`) for the interpreter title
`eme-bal` / `eme-bala` returns **25 occurrences, none of them with Meluhha.**
The regions the title is attested with here are:

| Region | Text | Period | Findspot | Custody | Museum no. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marhaši | P128649 | Ur III | Puzriš-Dagan (Drehem) | DePaul University, Chicago | Souvay 72 |
| Martu | P134754 | Ur III | Puzriš-Dagan (Drehem) | Musées royaux d'Art et d'Histoire, Brussels | MRAH O.0073 |
| Gutium | P215937 | Old Akkadian | Adab (Bismaya) | Oriental Institute, Chicago | OIM A01028 |

**This does not show that no interpreter of Meluhha is attested.** The corpus
searched is CDLI's *unblocked* ATF dump, weighted to administrative tablets;
seal legends are catalogued separately and are not necessarily in it. The
absence is typed as a property of the corpus.

What it does show is three things, and they change how the Meluhha instance
should be used:

1. **The title is generic.** Employing paid interpreters for eastern and
   northern neighbours was ordinary Ur III and Old Akkadian administrative
   practice. An interpreter of Meluhha is not remarkable in kind.
2. **The best-attested case here is Marhaši, not Meluhha** — in an
   administrative text, with a findspot, a custodian and a museum number.
3. **The Meluhha argument rests on a single object**, which is a
   source-independence problem rather than a body of evidence.

## What would clear it

Any one of: access to ORACC or CDLI's front end, which catalogue seal
inscriptions (`SRC-099`, `D-055`); access to the Louvre's collection database;
or the published edition supplied directly.

## What must not happen while it is held

The inscription must not be quoted, transliterated or translated from memory,
and its museum attribution must not be stated from recollection. It is exactly
the kind of well-known object where an unretrieved recollection would enter the
register wearing the authority of a retrieval — the failure `BF-031` records
avoiding for a date.
