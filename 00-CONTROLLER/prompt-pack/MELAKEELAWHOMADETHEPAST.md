# Who Made the Past?
## Ancient subaltern history and intellectual provenance
### Mela Keela / Veḷi — research foundation and feature programme

**Prepared 5 September 2026.** This programme treats ancient subaltern history as a core part of the museum’s research and visitor experience. It develops the supplied sixteen-section proposal, with particular attention to sections 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15 and 16.

**Institutional question:** Who produced knowledge, who carried it, what survived, who could authorize it, and who eventually received credit?

**Scope of this document:** A comparative synthesis of selected primary texts, original research and institutional collections, followed by implementable research and feature briefs. It is not a completed census of intellectual origins, an original-language corpus study, or an archaeological field investigation. Source access and unresolved work are identified in the source guide. The programme is recorded in the master work list; no production website changes or new historical claims have been published through this work.

## 1. The decision: make this a governing programme

Ancient subaltern history should shape the objects selected, the questions asked, the relationships recorded and the experiences built. A single page explaining archival bias cannot carry that responsibility. The programme belongs across archaeology, language, food, labour, religion, medicine, gender, ecology and living knowledge, as well as in a recognizable public collection called **Who Made the Past?**

The purpose is to make historical contribution and historical visibility separately investigable. Someone may have made a thing without naming it, transmitted a practice without writing it, or preserved a text without originating every idea inside it. Conversely, a compiler, translator or commentator may contribute substantial new knowledge. The research must discover those roles rather than distribute civilizational ownership in advance.

Five commitments should govern the work:

- **Recover contribution where evidence permits.** Identify makers, teachers, labourers, translators, patrons, communities and institutions, including those obscured by later attribution.
- **Explain the production of silence.** Distinguish an unrecorded activity, an unpreserved record, an unexcavated context, an untranslated text and a deliberately suppressed account.
- **Investigate power concretely.** Ask who controlled access, payment, copying, classification and publication. Name documented exclusion or coercion directly.
- **Compare mechanisms across unequal situations.** Apply the same evidentiary questions without pretending that different regimes possessed equal power or caused equal harm.
- **Keep the museum inside the inquiry.** Mela Keela’s titles, source choices, translations, diagrams and omissions also construct a past. They need revisable evidence records.

The central distinction is **symmetry of method, not symmetry of responsibility**. A colonial institution’s documented seizure and a community’s imperfect recollection are not equivalent because both require scrutiny. Equally, sympathy for a marginalized claim does not establish its chronology or transmission route.

This programme also needs a positive account of making: skill, experimentation, teaching, adaptation, care and collective invention. Subaltern people must not appear only when they suffer, disappear or are acted upon.

## 2. What the supplied proposal gets right—and what needs correction

| Proposition | Finding and operational correction |
|---|---|
| The surviving language does not establish the origin of every idea | Adopt. Record language of attestation separately from proposed origin, transmission and later attribution. |
| Sanskrit and Sanskritic authority should be distinguished | Adopt. Specify institutions, periods, texts and actors. A language is neither a single social agent nor a permanent political regime. |
| Ancient evidence has lower resolution | Revise. Resolution varies by question, place and medium, not just age. A document may expose named workers while a much later archive erases them. |
| Survival compounds power | Adopt as a research hypothesis with documented mechanisms. The illustrative 100-to-1 funnel is not historical data and must never become a factual chart. |
| Sanskritic and colonial knowledge systems can be compared | Adopt at the level of mechanisms. Establish differences in coercion, territorial power, institutions, technology and scale within each comparison. |

| Intellectual provenance is a new institutional asset | Adopt the asset; avoid claiming invention of the field. Translation history, book history, histories of science and community provenance already investigate these questions. |
| Synthesis may be more revealing than theft | Retain synthesis, but do not let it erase appropriation. Creative transformation and coercive acquisition can occur together. |

| No ideology receives exemption | Adopt as a procedure. Compare actual claims and evidence, not a ritual list implying every nationalist project is identical. |

The strongest revised formulation is: **Knowledge can be collectively made, unequally preserved, creatively transformed and unfairly credited—all at once.** These are separate questions, not competing descriptions from which the museum must select only one.

## 3. The intellectual foundations—and their limits

Ranajit Guha’s opening intervention in *Subaltern Studies I* challenged colonial and bourgeois-nationalist histories that made elites the principal agents of Indian politics. It provides a starting question about historical agency, not a ready-made classification of people in 2000 BCE. [Guha, 1982](https://www.cscsarchive.org/dataarchive/otherfiles/TA001125/file)

Spivak complicates the promise of recovering a voice: being represented in scholarship is not the same as being able to enter an institution’s circuits of recognition on one’s own terms. A museum cannot solve this by writing sympathetic dialogue for an unnamed ancient woman. It can distinguish her material traces, the historian’s inference and an explicitly invented reconstruction. [Spivak and the subsequent debate](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/can-the-subaltern-speak/9780231512855/)

Chakrabarty’s *Provincializing Europe* challenges the universalization of European historical categories. For this programme, the implication is to test concepts such as “religion,” “science,” “classical” and “modern” against particular histories rather than assuming a single ladder of development. This does not require rejecting comparison or European scholarship. [Chakrabarty, publisher abstract and chapter structure](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt7rsx9)

Trouillot locates silencing at multiple moments: producing sources, assembling archives, producing narratives and assigning retrospective significance. This is more useful than a simple written-versus-unwritten divide. A record can survive physically while remaining unavailable, untranscribed, unrecognized or excluded from a syllabus. [Trouillot, original excerpt](https://nyu.manifoldapp.org/read/silencing-the-past-excerpt-trulliot/section/2f251038-7193-4e95-aba1-01953ed871c3)

Hartman’s “Venus in Two Acts” makes the violence and limits of an archive central to the account itself. Its methodological relevance is the refusal to mistake narrative satisfaction for recovery. Mela Keela should not present speculative interior lives as information extracted from archaeological evidence. [Hartman, 2008](https://mumbletheoryhome.files.wordpress.com/2019/04/hartman-2008.pdf)

The programme must not imply that concern with subordinated lives began in 1982 or arrived only through university theory. Ambedkar’s *Annihilation of Caste* supplies an earlier, direct anti-caste intervention; its cancelled speaking invitation and subsequent publication also make institutional permission to speak unusually concrete. Build a wider primary-text strand around anti-caste thinkers and organizers rather than treating them as precursors in somebody else’s intellectual genealogy. [Ambedkar, annotated original text](https://ccnmtl.columbia.edu/projects/mmt/ambedkar/web/readings/aoc_print_2004.pdf)

Uma Chakravarti’s analysis of Brahmanical patriarchy brings caste, gender and control of reproduction into the same analytical frame. The museum should test normative prescriptions against other evidence, not assume that a rulebook transparently describes everyone’s conduct. [Chakravarti, 1993](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4399556)

**Working definition:** “Ancient subaltern” identifies a research problem about unequal power and representation. It does not name one biological population, one unchanging caste, or an automatically recoverable collective consciousness. Class, gender, servitude, dependency and exclusion may intersect differently in different contexts.

## 4. Historical resolution: replace the fading timeline

The proposed exhibit “the further back we go, the less we can see” is memorable but too categorical. Antiquity does not reduce every kind of visibility at the same rate. A better question is **What can these traces tell us—and about whom?**

The Turin Strike Papyrus records a labour dispute among royal workmen in ancient Egypt. It provides a documentary point of entry into workers’ claims that is unavailable for many other ancient populations. It does not make those unusually documented workers representative of all Egyptian labour. [Museo Egizio, Cat. 1880](https://collezioni.museoegizio.it/en-GB/material/Cat_1880)

For the Indus, an undeciphered script severely constrains access to named concepts, arguments and self-descriptions. It does not eliminate evidence about manufacturing, settlement, food processing, exchange or environmental conditions. Green’s argument for comparatively egalitarian Indus organization is itself a research interpretation to examine; it is not proof that hierarchy was absent or that all inhabitants occupied one subordinate position. [Green, “Killing the Priest-King”](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/30d7102d-e68f-4d10-baf5-6705e2672b65)

Pottery lipid analysis offers a different window: residues can inform questions about substances processed in sampled vessels. That is evidence about activities under analytical constraints, not a transcript of a cook’s identity, caste, beliefs or complete diet. [Cambridge research team on Indus lipid analysis](https://www.arch.cam.ac.uk/news/whats-cooking-analysis-fatty-residues-ancient-pottery-sheds-light-food-habits-indus)

| Dimension | Question for every case | Display rule |
|---|---|---|
| Temporal | How closely can the event or object be dated? | Show a range and method; separate composition, copying and deposition. |
| Spatial | Is this a findspot, production place or later repository? | Do not let one map pin represent all three. |
| Material | Which activities leave recognizable traces here? | Explain what the method detects and misses. |
| Linguistic | Can the text be read, translated and interpreted? | Separate decipherment, translation and semantic disagreement. |
| Social | Whose conduct or speech is recorded? | Identify the recording institution and represented population. |
| Transmission | How far is this copy from the proposed original? | Show intervening copies and missing links. |

These dimensions should be qualitative, evidenced descriptions initially. Do not invent a “72% historical visibility” score. A single score would conceal the exact limitation the feature is meant to teach.

**Absence protocol:** Specify the proposed activity, its expected trace, whether that trace could survive, whether appropriate contexts were sampled, whether the method could detect it, and plausible alternative explanations. An absent inscription in an unexcavated landscape has different force from a systematically absent feature in a well-specified, well-sampled corpus. Neither should become automatic proof of the preferred history.

## 5. How survival compounds—and sometimes reverses—power

The research should track creation, access, transmission, preservation, collection, cataloguing, translation, canonization and reuse. These are not independent percentage losses. Some traditions cross between media; a manuscript may re-enter attention after centuries; an inscription may become newly legible; a practice may persist without its former name.

An essential counterexample to a simple oral/subaltern versus written/elite opposition is Vedic recitation. Institutionalized oral transmission can be elaborate and authoritative. UNESCO’s account describes specialized techniques used to preserve the recitation tradition. Orality alone therefore cannot identify marginality, instability or absence of institutional support. [UNESCO, Vedic chanting](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/tradition-of-vedic-chanting-00062)

The dated Diamond Sutra copy from Dunhuang offers another useful separation. Its colophon dates this printed object to 11 May 868 and names a donor. The date of the surviving copy is not the date the teaching originated; sponsorship is not authorship. Its surviving physical form also makes environmental preservation part of the history. [International Dunhuang Programme, Diamond Sutra](https://idp.bl.uk/discover/learning/buddhism-on-the-silk-roads/articles/buddhism-on-the-ground/buddhist-texts-the-diamond-sutra/)

A collection should distinguish four mechanisms that are often collapsed:

- **Non-recording:** an activity was never represented in the relevant medium.
- **Non-survival:** a record was made but did not persist; the cause may be unknown.
- **Exclusion from recognition:** a surviving source was not catalogued, translated, funded or taught.
- **Documented suppression or destruction:** evidence identifies an intervention and, where possible, its agents and purpose.

Calling every absence destruction invents an event. Calling documented destruction “natural loss” conceals an event. The museum needs enough precision to avoid both.

A visitor should be able to follow a real object or text through these stages and ask how its visibility changed. Hypothetical sliders can explain mechanisms only if clearly labeled as simulations with user-chosen assumptions. Their output must never resemble a measured reconstruction of how much ancient knowledge was lost.

The same requirement applies to apparent corroboration. Ten modern accounts repeating one colonial translation are not ten independent witnesses. The ledger should expose shared source ancestry and distinguish independent observation from citation multiplication.

## 6. Compare languages without turning them into civilizations

The proposed English–Sanskrit analogy opens the right question, but it is only the beginning. Every comparison should identify whether it concerns **linguistic genealogy, documented borrowing, institutional function, or later classification**. Those relationships are not interchangeable.

### Sanskrit and Greek: kinship is one question; transmission is another

William Jones’s 1786 discourse argued from similarities in roots and grammar toward a common source for Sanskrit, Greek and Latin. It also contains aesthetic judgments about their relative refinement. The linguistic comparison and the prestige judgment should be displayed separately: neither proves racial identity or establishes who originated every idea written in those languages. [Jones, original discourse](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/reader/1-wm-jones)

Astral science offers a more bounded transmission case. Bill Mak’s re-examination of the *Yavanajātaka* challenges influential readings and dating arguments through manuscript evidence. This makes it especially useful for showing how a confident transmission chronology can depend on a fragile textual reconstruction. The visitor should compare the relevant readings and arguments, not receive a replacement certainty. [Mak, 2013, original article](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/292140116_The_date_and_nature_of_Sphujidhvaja%27s_Yavanajataka_reconsidered_in_the_light_of_some_newly_discovered_materials)

For grammar, compare actual intellectual tasks. Pāṇinian derivational rules and the *Technē Grammatikē* traditionally attributed to Dionysius Thrax should not be judged as though both were the same kind of handbook. The latter’s opening programme includes reading, explanation, etymology, analogy and literary criticism. Its received text and attribution also require specialist historical treatment. Computational implementation of portions of Pāṇini demonstrates productive formal structure; it does not by itself prove a civilization-wide superiority or that Sanskrit was a programming language. [Received Greek text in translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_grammar_of_Dionysios_Thrax); [Krishna and Goyal’s computational study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.05670)

### Greek and Latin: political power does not settle intellectual prestige

In the opening of *De finibus*, Cicero addresses readers who regard Greek philosophical subjects as ill-suited to Latin treatment. He defends writing philosophy in Latin and explains his own intellectual work. This is an unusually direct source for language prestige, audience formation and adaptation. Latin composition here need not mean either wholly Roman invention or passive copying. [Cicero, Book I, opening sections](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Finibus/1%2A.html)

A Greek–Latin–Sanskrit comparison can therefore ask who could claim authority in a prestigious inherited medium, who defended another language’s capacity, and what changed through adaptation. It should not presume identical institutions or a universal transition from a “sacred” to a “secular” language.

### Babylonian and Greek astronomy: make the technical transfer visible

Alexander Jones examines the adaptation of Babylonian methods in Greek numerical astronomy. This supplies a concrete counterexample to histories that equate the surviving canonical language with an isolated origin. The relevant unit is a particular numerical method, parameter or procedure—not “Greek science” as one indivisible achievement. [Jones, 1991, original article](https://archive.nyu.edu/handle/2451/49537)

### Greek, Syriac and Arabic: translators as intellectual workers

Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq’s account of his Galen translations is an appropriate primary-source anchor for the next comparative case. The published edition provides a route into identifying translation work rather than treating “Arabic transmission” as an anonymous pipe. Exact claims about particular collaborators, textual repairs and routes should be checked against the edition before the exhibit is written. Arabic intellectual production must not be reduced to preservation of Greek material. [Ḥunayn, edition catalogue](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/bfx8kyea)

### English today: useful analogy, separate research needed

The language of a publication is not a sufficient identifier of every contributor’s intellectual ancestry. That conceptual point needs no invented statistic. A contemporary exhibit, however, should use a bounded corpus of papers and actual contributor, institution, funding and editorial data. Nationality, ethnicity, language and workplace must remain separate variables. Modern publishing may illuminate mechanisms of prestige and access without being declared equivalent to ancient educational exclusion.

## 7. Two documentary pilots that can carry the programme

### Hortus Malabaricus: local expertise inside a Latin botanical work

The seventeenth-century *Hortus Malabaricus* offers unusually concrete evidence of multiple contributors, languages and unequal institutions. Research on the work identifies Itty Achudan’s contribution alongside Brahmin certifiers and European participants; surviving certificates make parts of the attribution problem visible within the publication itself. The case resists both “Europe discovered these plants” and an equally flattening story of one undifferentiated local community. [Max Planck Institute research account](https://www.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/feature-story/malabaricus-botanical-seventeenth-century); [Magdalen College’s examination of the book](https://www.magd.ox.ac.uk/blog/hortus-malabaricus-a-botanical-and-linguistic-treasure/)

**Build question:** What work did each person perform, what evidence names them, and what did the book’s later circulation make visible? Compare the title page, contributor certificates, a bounded plant entry and later attribution. Ask separately about expertise, translation, authorization and benefit. Do not infer the origin of every recorded botanical practice from the surviving contributor list.

### Florentine Codex: parallel columns are not equivalent voices

Getty’s research on the Florentine Codex identifies named Nahua participants and distinguishes its Nahuatl, Spanish and pictorial narratives. Its account shows that the Spanish is not simply a transparent equivalent of the Nahuatl. This creates an excellent case for asking what an apparently multilingual publication preserves, transforms or omits. [Richter and Houtrouw, Getty research essay](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/resources/1_Richter_Houtrouw)

**Build question:** What changes when the visitor reads one language, another, and the image? Select one short passage whose differences have been established by qualified scholars. Credit translators and contributors; show the original alongside the translation where rights allow. Do not imply that all differences are deliberate censorship, or that a single manuscript represents every Nahua perspective.

**Why pair them:** Both make transmission and contribution inspectable through surviving documents. Their institutions, purposes, local power relations and production histories differ. The comparison should reveal those differences rather than award either work a single “colonial” score. These documentary cases can validate the provenance interaction before a much less resolvable ancient genealogy is attempted.

## 8. Sanskritic authority, empire and the contradiction of admiration

Pollock’s account of the Sanskrit cosmopolis provides a framework for examining Sanskrit’s literary and political reach and the emergence of regional literary cultures. It is a historical model to test against dated inscriptions, texts and patrons. It should not become the claim that all Sanskrit writing served one institution or that every regional practice entered through one process. [Pollock, publisher account](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-language-of-the-gods-in-the-world-of-men/paper)

Use **Sanskritization** carefully. Srinivas’s technical sociological concept concerns status mobility through adoption of higher-status practices. Translation into Sanskrit, Brahmanical legitimation, renaming a place, editing a medical text and restricting education may overlap, but they are not synonyms. Record the mechanism actually evidenced rather than using one term to prejudge all of them.

The Greater Magadha thesis is a strong investigation candidate precisely because its chronology and explanatory reach remain debated. The production task is to collate relevant passages, distinguish textual strata and compare explicit arguments about karma, rebirth and liberation. “Absent from the earliest selected corpus” and “invented by an identified non-Vedic population” are different claims. [Wynne’s scholarly review of Bronkhorst](https://networks.h-net.org/node/6060/reviews/16094/wynne-bronkhorst-greater-magadha-studies-culture-early-india)

Morrison’s 2025 review identifies colonial inheritances in South Asian archaeology, including textual priority, singular civilizational narratives and essentialized social categories. That supports investigating the discipline’s institutional history. It does not establish that every excavator or every use of a text reproduces the same bias. [Morrison, review abstract](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-anthro-072623-030252)

Trautmann’s account is especially valuable for the apparent contradiction between admiration and racial hierarchy. His analysis describes how linguistic kinship and admiration for Indian intellectual achievement came into conflict with Victorian racial assumptions, and how a racialized account of civilization attempted to reconcile them. Admiration did not necessarily dissolve domination; it could coexist with selective appropriation and exclusion. [Trautmann, publisher abstract](https://academic.oup.com/california-scholarship-online/book/22475)

| Comparative axis | What must be specified in each case |
|---|---|
| Authority | Who could certify a text, practice, translation or scholarly claim? |
| Access | Who could learn, teach, copy, participate or dissent—and with what evidence? |
| Material power | Patronage, land, employment, institutions, coercion and property arrangements. |
| Representation | How were local terms, people and practices translated or classified? |
| Credit | Who was named, anonymized, reclassified or remembered later? |
| Agency | Who negotiated, refused, adapted, benefited, redirected or resisted? |
| Scale and alternatives | What varied within the system, and which competing institutions existed? |

This is the appropriate comparison between Sanskritic and colonial knowledge systems. It investigates repeated mechanisms while retaining differences in historical formation and responsibility.

## 9. Aryan racialization: a branching history, not an inevitable chain

The museum should retain this subject centrally. It should also disentangle an ancient self-designation, a philological classification, an ethnological claim and a racial-political category. They cannot be treated as one stable meaning travelling unchanged through time.

The US Holocaust Memorial Museum explains how “Aryan” was falsely racialized and incorporated into Nazi ideology. Its account distinguishes a linguistic designation from the invented racial category. That distinction allows the museum to confront the political afterlife directly while preserving the validity of comparative linguistic evidence. [USHMM, “Aryan”](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/aryan-1)

**Feature form:** A branching concept history. Each node contains a dated use, author or institution, the meaning assigned, the population included or excluded, and an evidenced relationship to another use. Connect adoption, translation, criticism and legal classification with different relations. Antisemitism, racial anthropology, nationalism and state institutions require their own documented inputs; they must not disappear behind one arrow from Sanskrit.

**Research sequence:** Establish a small set of primary editions and institutional documents; identify exactly which earlier writers later actors cited; distinguish citation from causal influence; commission specialist review of the Nazi and Holocaust material; then add contemporary political appropriations as separately evidenced cases.

**Learning requirement:** Visitors should leave able to explain why related languages do not establish a pure race. Avoid interfaces that ask children to sort human beings into racial types, award points for extremist categories, or turn persecution into an aesthetic spectacle. Public and classroom versions can examine how a classification acquired consequences without reproducing its authority.

The same discipline must govern modern nationalism. Choose a specific textbook edition, museum label, public claim or policy corpus. Compare its language with the underlying evidence and earlier versions. Do not substitute a list of ideological labels for analysis of what was actually asserted or changed.

## 10. Intellectual provenance: the shared data model

The museum needs two connected but distinct histories: **custody of a physical record** and **transmission of an idea or practice**. An object can change hands without an idea being transmitted; a method can travel without the original manuscript travelling.

A minimum model should contain:

| Record | Necessary fields |
|---|---|
| Attestation | Exact passage or observation; source ID and locator; original language; edition; date range and dating basis. |
| Idea or practice | Bounded description; variants; scope; distinction from a modern category imposed retrospectively. |
| Agent | Person, group or institution; evidenced role; identification uncertainty; preferred attribution where applicable. |
| Contribution | Making, teaching, testing, collecting, translating, editing, commenting, codifying, funding or publishing. |
| Transmission claim | Proposed relation between attestations or practices; mechanism; chronology; alternatives; supporting and opposing evidence. |
| Visibility event | Copying, collection, cataloguing, translation, publication, curriculum adoption, digitization or rediscovery. |
| Rights and consent | Source/media permissions; restrictions on contributed knowledge; allowed displays and reuse. |
| Review and revision | Claim status; reviewer role; unresolved questions; version; downstream displays affected by correction. |

**Bridges are claims.** Resemblance is not automatically transmission. Priority is not automatically influence. Shared vocabulary is not automatically a shared institution. Population affinity is not automatically language, religion or modern community identity.

Evidence roles belong to the evidence-to-claim relation: supports, contradicts, contextualizes, dates, localizes, identifies or cannot discriminate. Each relation should explain the argumentative work performed. Source reliability, inference strength, dating precision and scholarly disagreement must remain separately inspectable.

Do not implement one flat scale reading “documented, inferred, plausible, contested, speculative, unknown.” A claim can be well documented and contested. A document can exist while its interpretation is uncertain. Record at least **evidence basis**, **assessment of the inference**, **disagreement**, and **review status** separately; simplify their presentation in context.

The existing principle “the record is the unit of truth” is useful operationally if it means an auditable, versioned record. It must not imply that entering a proposition into the ledger makes it true. The investigation remains the unit of experience; the record remains the unit that can be inspected and corrected.

**Do not compute a civilizational ownership percentage.** A denominator such as “all Sanskrit knowledge” is not established, and ideas cannot necessarily be divided into comparable units. Begin with bounded cases and report what the case set includes and excludes.

## 11. Eight priority features

These features directly implement the sections the owner singled out. They reuse WITNESS, contextual PROVE IT, TIME·PLACE and Field Bag rather than requiring eight new engines.

### F1 — What Can We Know? · supplied section 4

**Visitor question:** Why can we know what someone cooked but not what language they spoke?

**Interaction:** Compare two or three evidence packets; choose which questions each can answer; reveal the method and limit. Start with Indus material evidence and an unusually documentary ancient case. Do not rank civilizations by one visibility score.

**Research dependency:** Reviewed contexts, dating methods, original records and method-specific limits. **Acceptance:** A visitor can distinguish an observation, an inference and an unanswered question; the interface never reveals an identity unsupported by the packet.

### F2 — How a Past Survives · supplied section 5

**Visitor question:** Why is this record here when so much else is missing?

**Interaction:** Follow one real text or object through copying, storage, collection, translation and later attention. At a missing stage, offer competing explanations where evidence permits. A separate hypothetical exercise may test preservation assumptions.

**Research dependency:** A documented custody/visibility history, with genuine gaps retained. **Acceptance:** The visitor can distinguish loss, non-recording and exclusion; every numerical display has a source or an explicit simulation label.

### F3 — The Same Move, Different Power · supplied section 7

**Visitor question:** What changes when someone else supplies the authoritative name?

**Interaction:** Two cases share comparison prompts—authority, access, transformation, credit, benefit—while retaining different timelines and institutions. Begin with documentary pilots; expand to Sanskritic textualization after case-level research.

**Research dependency:** Evidence for the specific mechanism in both cases, including asymmetries and counterexamples. **Acceptance:** Visitors can name one meaningful similarity and one consequential difference. Neither side is assigned a generic oppression score.

### F4 — Lives Without Names · supplied section 9

**Visitor question:** Who did the work that made this object possible?

**Interaction:** Reconstruct a production sequence: obtaining material, preparing it, manufacturing, transport and use. Attach evidence to each operation and inspect which roles cannot be assigned to named people.

**Research dependency:** Archaeological context, experimental or analytical methods, and competing interpretations of labour organization. **Acceptance:** No operation is silently assigned a caste, gender, ethnicity or legal status. When such an assignment is defensible, its distinct evidence is shown.

### F5 — Admired, Then Racialized · supplied section 11

**Visitor question:** How could admiration for a language coexist with exclusion of its speakers?

**Interaction:** Compare short, dated passages and institutional classifications. Trace changes in meaning through a branching concept history, with accessible explanations of linguistic kinship versus racial ideology.

**Research dependency:** Primary editions, exact locators and specialist review of racialization and Holocaust history. **Acceptance:** Visitors do not leave believing that comparative linguistics proves racial hierarchy or that every philologist shared one political project.

### F6 — Who Made the Past? · supplied section 13

**Visitor question:** Where would you like to begin—with work, a word, an object, an idea or a missing record?

**Interaction:** A curated collection connecting the other features to existing exhibits and Living Worlds. Suggested journeys should emerge from records and questions; researchers can descend to the ledger.

**Research dependency:** Verified route inventory and editorial mapping. **Acceptance:** The collection adds meaningful pathways rather than reproducing the research index or creating twenty thin pages for twenty proposed room titles.

### F7 — Who Gets the Credit? · supplied section 15

**Visitor question:** Who contributed to this idea, and why is only one name remembered?

**Interaction:** Build a contribution graph from a bounded case. Switch between attestation, contribution, transmission and later attribution. Inspect evidence for each bridge; save a defensible attribution statement in Field Bag.

**Research dependency:** Source-level contribution records and contested links. **Acceptance:** The graph distinguishes author, translator, sponsor and earlier practitioner; an unknown predecessor stays unknown.

### F8 — Many Hands, Unequal Credit · supplied section 16

**Visitor question:** Can something be a creative synthesis and an unfair transaction?

**Interaction:** Examine transformation, access, consent, attribution and reward on separate axes. Construct a short conclusion using evidence; compare it with a reviewed explanation of what is supported and unresolved.

**Research dependency:** Evidence of both intellectual work and institutional relationships. **Acceptance:** “Synthesis” never automatically clears coercion, and “appropriation” never substitutes for specifying what happened.

The original twenty-room wing is a valuable intellectual outline. Implement it as five collections with expandable investigations, not a Year-1 commitment to twenty bespoke experiences.

## 14. Research operations, accountability and build sequence

### A case dossier before a feature

Every investigation needs a bounded question, a source list, original-language passages or analytical observations, a chronology, contribution/relationship claims, alternatives, rights and a record of review. For textual cases, distinguish composition, redaction, manuscript date, edition and translation. For archaeological cases, distinguish observation, sample context, analytical result and social interpretation.

A claim should move through draft, checked, reviewed, published, revised or withdrawn states. The person who writes the public story should not be its only scholarly verifier. Review should match the claim: a linguist for language correspondence, an archaeologist for context, an appropriate historian for institutional genealogy, and knowledge holders for contributed living practice. These are functions to commission, not a promise of a large permanent council.

Community authority should cover restricted or contributed knowledge, privacy, attribution and consent conditions. It should have a documented scope and route for resolving disputes. It does not imply an unlimited veto over discussion of publicly available historical evidence. Pay for expertise and participation; do not treat a community’s presence as free authentication of the museum’s narrative.

Pitt Rivers describes changes to displays, interpretation and consultation in response to the colonial histories and effects of its collections. Wereldmuseum explicitly makes its own institutional involvement in colonial representation part of an exhibition. These are useful precedents, not proof that either institution has completed decolonization. Mela Keela can study their choices and assess its own outcomes. [Pitt Rivers, Critical Changes](https://www.prm.ox.ac.uk/critical-changes); [Wereldmuseum, institutional account](https://amsterdam.wereldmuseum.nl/en/whats-on/exhibitions/our-colonial-inheritance/why-exhibition-about-our-colonial-inheritance)

Soneji’s work on devadāsī memory and modernity provides a research lead for the Body and Work worlds: embodied knowledge, displacement and remembered practice need treatment beyond textual canons. Build a specific documented case before asserting continuous descent from ancient performance. [Soneji, publisher account](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/U/bo12120868.html)

### Sequence by dependency, not by page count

1. **Reconcile the asset base.** Match relevant branch records, live routes, source files and existing claims; retain corrections already made.
2. **Model two documentary cases.** Use a bounded Hortus entry/contributor set and a reviewed Florentine passage to test contribution, translation and visibility records.
3. **Build one complete investigation.** WITNESS, contextual PROVE IT and Field Bag should support inspecting a claim, comparing evidence, forming a conclusion and retaining the result.
4. **Add comparative views and TIME·PLACE.** Reuse the same records; label each connection as transmission, shared source, analogy or disputed relation.
5. **Add an ancient material case.** Test the system against unnamed work, incomplete chronology and nontextual evidence; use F1 and F4 before attempting a philosophical origin claim.
6. **Commission deeper specialist lanes.** Greek–Sanskrit astral science, grammar across traditions, karma/rebirth chronology, racialization and embodied knowledge each need their own bounded dossier.
7. **Expand the collection after reuse is demonstrated.** Add routes when they answer a distinct question, not to fill the twenty-room outline mechanically.

### Measures that matter

Measure whether visitors distinguish attestation from origin, identify an unsupported bridge, recognize a contribution other than authorship, and state one consequential limit. Test whether they can also recognize a well-supported conclusion: uncertainty literacy must not become the belief that nothing can be known.

For production, record research hours, specialist review, rights work, translation, authoring effort, corrections and the cost of a second case using the same interaction. Do not invent a programme budget before these inputs exist. Separate founder labour from cash, and avoid funding a large engine suite before the content workflow has been tested.

AI can assist extraction, cross-reference discovery, draft transformations, translation comparison and code. Human review must authorize historical interpretation, final translation, rights and community representation. Generated reconstructions require visible separation from historical evidence. No model output becomes a source merely because another model repeats it.

### Research lanes still requiring original work

**South Asian textual histories:** Collate the proposed karma/rebirth passages across Vedic, Buddhist and Jain sources; separate attestation from reconstructed chronology. Examine specific Sanskrit and vernacular cases of naming, commentary and incorporation. Do not infer appropriation from the language of a later record alone.

**Comparative grammar:** Select matched tasks in Pāṇini, the Greek grammatical tradition and Latin grammarians; include chronology and textual transmission. Compare the problem solved, assumptions, notation, audience and pedagogy. Counts of rules or words cannot by themselves rank intellectual sophistication.

**Transmission beyond the first pilots:** Develop Greek–Syriac–Arabic and Sanskrit–Persian dossiers; verify named translators, patrons, manuscript witnesses and innovations. The Persian lane is retained as a research requirement, not represented here as a completed case study.

**Ancient labour and material life:** Select sites and artifact classes with sufficient context to investigate craft, food preparation, resource extraction and bodily consequences of work. Health, isotope or skeletal observations must not be converted automatically into occupation, ethnicity or social identity.

**Archive access and canon formation:** Compare actual catalogues, editions, collecting decisions and syllabi over specified periods. Establish which alternatives were available to selectors. A missing modern translation is a different problem from the destruction of an ancient text.

**Contemporary publishing:** Build a defined sample before making quantitative claims about English, institutional concentration, editorial access or attribution. Report the denominator and limitations; do not use nationality as a proxy for intellectual origin.

**Anti-caste and community intellectual histories:** Extend beyond academic Subaltern Studies through primary writings, organizational records and consented testimony. Do not arrange diverse thinkers and communities as one inevitable lineage leading to Mela Keela.

The intended outcome is a museum in which a visitor can follow not only an object’s journey but also the work, institutions and interpretations that made it legible. The most demanding question is not simply who owned the past. It is **what we can establish about all the people who made it—and how our own account gives them different degrees of visibility.**
