## 2. Transliteration

`púr-`, cited from the Zürich lemmatisation in ISO-15919. The Saṃhitā and the
padapāṭha differ where sandhi is undone: RV 1.58.8d writes Saṃhitā `pūrbhíḥ`
against padapāṭha `pūḥ-bhiḥ`, which is the ancient Śākalya analysis confirming
the stem boundary.

## 3. Grammatical form

A **feminine root noun**, stem `púr-`, declined athematically. All 83 simplex
tokens, from `03-REGISTERS/rigveda-pur-family-occurrences.csv`:

| Form | Case | n | Share |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| `púraḥ` | ACC.PL | 45 | 54.2% |
| `púram` | ACC.SG | 11 | 13.3% |
| `purā́m` / `púrām` | GEN.PL | 10 | 12.0% |
| `pūrbhíḥ` | INS.PL | 6 | 7.2% |
| `pū́ḥ` | NOM.SG | 5 | 6.0% |
| `púraḥ` | NOM.PL | 2 | 2.4% |
| `purí` | LOC.SG | 2 | 2.4% |
| `purā́` | INS.SG | 1 | 1.2% |
| `pūrṣú` | LOC.PL | 1 | 1.2% |

Seven derivatives complete the family: `puraṃdará-` (11), `pūrbhíd-` incl.
`pūrbhíttama-` (8), `pūrbhídya-`, `pū́rpati-`, `purohán-`, `púrya-` (1 each).

**Two facts in this table do work later.**

- **67.5% of tokens are accusative.** The commonest thing a *púr* does in the
  Rigveda is be the direct object of a verb. The roots most often in the same
  hemistich as an accusative `púr-` are √bhid- (11), √dr̥̄- (10), √han- (8),
  √dr̥h- (7), √ruj- (5) — and Griffith, Geldner and Grassmann render these
  passages with *demolish, break, shatter, rend, crush* / *brechen, zerstören,
  zerschmettern*.
- **Three tokens of 83 are locative.** `purí` twice, `pūrṣú` once. A *púr* is
  almost never a place where anybody is said to be. That is 3.6% of the
  simplex, and it is the single most awkward number for any reading that makes
  *púr-* a settlement.

## 4. Semantic range

**Grassmann's own definition** (`SRC-026`), the gloss that fixed this
register's family boundary:

> `púr-` — **Wall aus Steinen und Lehm, Verschanzung, Palisade**
> *"wall of stones and clay, entrenchment, palisade"*

This is worth pausing on. The standard nineteenth-century Rigveda lexicon does
**not** define the word as a city, a town, or even a fortress. It defines it as
an **earthwork**: a wall, a rampart, a palisade. Grassmann's related glosses run
the same way — `puraṃdará-` "Zerstörer der **Wälle**", `pūrbhíd-` "die **Wälle**
aufbrechend", `purohán-` "die **Wälle** zerschlagend", `pū́rpati-` "Herr der
**Burg**", `púrya-` "in einem festen Platz befindlich".

The corpus's own qualifiers, from `03-REGISTERS/rigveda-pur-fields.csv`, are
consistent with an enclosure rather than a settlement: `āyasá-` "of metal" (8),
`aśmanmáya-` "of stone" (1), `āmá-` "raw, unbaked" (1), `śā́rada-` "autumnal"
(3), `pr̥thú-` "broad", `urú-` "wide", `bahulá-` "thick", `śatábhuji-`
"hundredfold", `ádhr̥ṣṭa-` "unassailable", `dvā́r-` "door", and — at RV 8.1.28 —
`cariṣṇú-` **"moving"**. Śuṣṇa's *púr* moves.

## 5. Textual context

`púr-` is not evenly distributed across kinds of passage. Of the ten passages
that state a material, **seven of the eight metal ones are protective, mythic
or metaphorical** and only RV 2.20.8 is an enemy's (`PUR4J-013`). At RV 7.15.14
Agni is asked **to be** a *púr*; at RV 7.95.1 the river Sarasvatī **is** one; at
RV 4.27.1 and 8.100.8 a hundred metal *púr*s **confine** a bird; at RV 1.58.8,
7.3.7 and 7.16.10 a god is asked to protect the singer **with** them; at
RV 10.101.8 priests are told to **make** them, next to stitching armour and
building a cattle-pen; at RV 8.6.23 a *púr* is a **simile** (`púraṁ ná`).

## 7. The translation used

**None, as a default.** This register cites `púr-` by its transliteration and
its locator. Where an English word is unavoidable, the reason is given on the
row. That is not fastidiousness: §8 below is why.

## 8. Alternative translations

Across the 103 passages, the five translations with meaningful coverage. Counts
are of fort-words in the rendering of a `púr-` passage, by surface-string match
and not lemmatised, so they are indicative rather than exact — the Russian
figures especially.

| Translator | Date | Fort-words used |
|---|---|---|
| **Grassmann** | 1876–7 | *Burg* / *Burgen* 84 · *Feste(n)* 9 · **never *Stadt*** |
| **Griffith** | 1890 | *fort(s)* 63 · *castle(s)* 33 · *stronghold(s)* 5 · *fortress(es)* 4 · ***city* 1** |
| **Geldner** | 1951 | *Burg(en)* 79 · *Feste(n)* 9 · *Bollwerk* 1 · *Wall* 1 · **never *Stadt*** |
| **Renou** | 1955–64 | *forteresse(s)* 34 · *citadelle(s)* 22 (58 passages of 103) |
| **Elizarenkova** | 1989–99 | *крепость* overwhelmingly · *вал* · *твердыня* |

**No translator in the clone renders `púr-` as a city, with exactly one
exception in 103 passages.** Griffith at RV 1.173.10 renders `pū́rpati-` "lord
of a *púr*" as *"some city's lord"*. Geldner has *Burgherr* and Grassmann
*Burgherrn* at the same locator.

That single word is worth its own line. The one place where an English
translator reaches for "city" is the one place a *political office* attached to
a *púr* is named — precisely where the urban reading is most consequential.

**Dependency, not corroboration.** Grassmann's translation and the Grassmann
glosses that fixed this family are one man's judgement (`DEP-021`), and
Elizarenkova worked with Geldner in view (`DEP-023`). Five columns in that table
are not five witnesses.

### 8.1 The `āyasá-` crux, which is chronological

Systematic and unanimous within each translator, across all eight passages:

| | RV 1.58.8 | 2.20.8 | 4.27.1 | 7.3.7 | 7.15.14 | 7.95.1 | 8.100.8 | 10.101.8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Griffith | iron | iron | iron | iron | iron | iron | iron | iron |
| Geldner | ehern | ehern | ehern | ehern | ehern | ehern | ehern | ehern |
| Grassmann | Erz | ehern | **Eisen** | — | ehern | ehern | ehern | ehern |

Griffith says **iron** eight times out of eight. Geldner says **ehern** — *of
ore, brass, bronze* — eight times out of eight. They are describing different
metals, and neither argues the point in the text available here.

**This is the §7 case in its purest form.** The two metals have different
histories in South Asia, so which is meant bears on when the passages could
have been composed; a translator's single lexical habit can therefore move a
chronology. **No source retrieved in this unit settles it**, and nothing here
asserts either reading. It is recorded as an open crux with a named
consequence, which is what §7 requires of a translation choice that decides a
historical question.

## 9. What each translation choice does to the historical question

| Rendering | What it imports | What follows if adopted silently |
|---|---|---|
| **city, town** | permanent nucleated settlement, population, streets | The Rigveda becomes a record of urban destruction, and the step to a Mature Harappan identification is nearly automatic — the step §4J forbids taking automatically. Used **once** in 103 passages by one translator. |
| **castle** | a medieval European stone keep, with a lord and a garrison | Imports feudal Europe. Griffith's second commonest word (33), and the one carrying "nine-and-ninety castles" into anglophone circulation. |
| **fortress, fort** | a purpose-built military structure | Neutral on urbanism but not on function; assumes a work built to be defended, which the protective and metaphorical passages do not obviously describe. |
| **stronghold** | a defended place, unspecified | The least committed English option. |
| **rampart, wall, palisade, entrenchment** | an earthwork, possibly enclosing nothing permanent | **Grassmann's own definition.** No urbanism follows at all, and it fits `āmá-` "unbaked", `cariṣṇú-` "moving", and the 3.6% locative rate. |
| **Burg** | in German, a fortified seat — between "fort" and "castle" | The near-unanimous choice of both German translators, neither of whom ever writes *Stadt*. |

**The word does not decide the question, and it must not be allowed to.** The
corpus's own evidence — 67.5% accusative, 3.6% locative, seven of eight metal
*púr*s divine or protective, one of them a river and one a god, one of them
moving — is not what a vocabulary of cities would predict. That is a set of
measurements, and it constrains the typology in §4J without settling any single
passage.

## 10. The category audit §7 asks for

**"Fort" fails the audit as a default gloss.** It is serviceable for the
Śambara–Divodāsa passages, where something is besieged and broken. It is
actively misleading at RV 7.95.1 (a river), RV 7.15.14 (a god), RV 8.1.28 (a
*moving* one), and RV 8.6.23 (a simile). An English word carried unchanged
across all 103 passages would assert a uniformity the corpus does not have —
which is Version 12 line 1259's point, "Do not force every *pur* to mean an
urban city, one material or one historical event".

**A second category needs adding to §7's list.** The patron and opponent fields
in `03-REGISTERS/rigveda-pur-fields.csv` are sorted by Grassmann's category
**Dämon**, which is what places Śambara, Pipru, Śuṣṇa, Namuci, Varcin, Vaṅgṛda,
Kuyava, Cumuri and Dhuni on the opponent side. §7's list — race, tribe, slave,
barbarian, fort, religion, caste, civilization, invasion, indigenous — does not
include *demon*, and it is doing exactly the work §7 exists to catch: a
nineteenth-century German theological category deciding, before any passage is
read, that the holders of the forts were not people. Logged in
`04-AUDITS/REAUDIT-QUEUE.csv`.

A third is visible in the same lexicon and is not this unit's to pursue:
Grassmann glosses `ā́rya-` as "Angehöriger der drei oberen Grosskasten", which
imports the later caste system into a Rigvedic word. It surfaced here only as a
false positive in the numeral scan (§4.1 of the method note).

## 12. The audit does not stop at *púr-*, because the English word does not

*Added by the domain J unit, 2026-09-07. Register row: `DJ-I-03`;
measurements `DJ-005`, `DJ-010`, `DJ-011`, `DJ-012`; script
`04-AUDITS/rv-pur-nandi-gate.py`.*

§10 audits **fort** against `púr-`. But §7 audits an *English category*, and a
category is not audited by testing one Sanskrit word against it. The bracket
"fort" is wide, and at least three other Rigvedic words move in and out of it
depending on which century's lexicon is consulted.

| Word | The corpus's **Rigvedic** gloss (Zürich column, `SRC-089` — *not* Grassmann; `BF-017`) | Tokens | What the English bracket does |
|---|---|---:|---|
| `púr-` | Wall aus Steinen und Lehm, Verschanzung, Palisade | 83 | promotes an earthwork to a city |
| `dehī́-` | Aufwurf, Damm, Wall | 2 | leaves a plastered earth wall out of the corpus entirely |
| `durgá-` | unwegsamer, schwer begehbarer Ort, Gefahr | 21 | imports the *Classical* Sanskrit "fortress" into the Rigveda |
| `grā́ma-` | Treck, Heerhaufen, Kriegerschar, wehrhafte Mannschaft einer Siedlung, **Dorfgemeinde, bewohnter Platz** | 9 | the gloss carries *both* senses and does not decide between them |

**`durgá-` is the sharp case, and this section got it wrong in its first
version.** In Classical Sanskrit `durga-` is unambiguously a fortress, and
Nandi's abstract lists it among the Rigveda's words for fortified settlements
(`SRC-086`, itself on hold at `DJ-010B`). All 21 Rigvedic tokens are neuter,
and the *majority* mean difficult ground, rough country or peril — six of them
the same refrain at RV 1.106.1–6, *"as a chariot from a difficult ravine"*, and
the recurrent construction is a prayer to be *carried across* one.

**But the claim that none is ever held is false, and the falsifier fired at a
passage this brief itself listed.** RV 5.34.7, `durgé caná dhriyate víśva ā́
purú jáno`: Griffith *"not even in wide **stronghold** may all the folk **stand
firm**"*; Geldner *"in einer **Bergfeste hält sich** … ein ganzer
Volksstamm"*; Grassmann *"an **festverschlossnem Ort** … **hält nicht lange
Stand**"*. A fortified place, and the verb is holding out. RV 5.54.4 splits the
translators — Griffith "strongholds", Geldner "unwegsamen Gegenden", Grassmann
"Wildniss" — and RV 4.28.3 and 7.25.2 are unresolved. The base is thinner than
21 suggests: with the 1.106 refrain and the verbatim pair RV 7.60.12 = 7.61.7,
there are at most **15 distinct formulations**.

So the §7 point stands in a weaker and more interesting form. The danger is not
that `durga-` "fortress" is simply a later meaning read backwards; it is that
**the English bracket forces a choice the Rigvedic evidence does not make**,
and translators inside one tradition split on the same line. `DJ-011` is
`PROVISIONAL` and `RA-020` sends all 21 passages back to be read from an
edition rather than through a translation index.

**`dehī́-` is the case that costs a number.** RV 6.47.2 has Indra smash
`navatíṁ náva ca dehyò` — **ninety-nine** of them — and they are **Śambara's**,
the same opponent as three of the six `púr-` passages that state ninety-nine,
in the same expression. Griffith "ramparts", Geldner "Mauern", Grassmann
"Wälle", Elizarenkova "валы": all four render both the wall and the number. The
word is one of the six fort-adjacent lemmas `PUR-006` excludes **by design**,
on an etymological membership test. So the sentence "six Rigvedic passages say
ninety-nine forts" is true on the etymological reading of §4J's *complete
corpus* and false on the semantic one, where it is seven (`DJ-005`,
reconciliation C-2, queued as `RA-016`).

`dehī́-` derives from √*dih* "smear, plaster up" — a heaped and plastered earth
wall. Set beside the gloss column's `púr-` as a wall "of stones and clay", the
two Rigvedic wall-words converge on **earthwork** in that column, and neither
converges there on masonry, brick or city.

**That is an observation about one gloss column and it is contradicted inside
the same corpus.** Renou renders `púr-` *"forteresses (en briques) crues"* at
RV 2.35.6 and *"maître de la cité"* at RV 1.173.10 (`DJ-013`); and the corpus
carries `pakvá-` "gebacken" against `āmá-` "roh, ungekocht", putting the
unfired term on a fort (`DJ-009`). An earthwork of plastered mud and an
unfired-brick wall are not far apart, and nothing here decides between them.
`DEP-005`, `DEP-024`, `DEP-026`; `DJ-I-02` says what this can and cannot
bear.

**The correction is not a new default gloss.** Replacing "fort" with
"earthwork" across 103 passages would repeat §10's error in the other
direction — asserting a uniformity the corpus does not have, and doing it on a
single nineteenth-century lexicon. What the audit yields is narrower and
harder: the English bracket has to be dropped per passage, and each of these
four words needs its own §7 block before it is translated. Only `púr-` has one,
above.
