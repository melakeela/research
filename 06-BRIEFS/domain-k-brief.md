## QUESTION

The Indus cities left inscribed objects — how many is a question this
record cannot answer, because the corpus publication was unreachable — and
no one can read them. The Rigveda, composed in a region that overlaps
theirs, never unambiguously describes writing. Does the second fact tell
us anything about the first?

Whether "afterwards" is the right word is itself unsettled here: no
absolute date for either side was retrievable, so this record cannot place
the two in sequence, only note that both are conventionally placed in the
same broad region.

## WHAT IS OBSERVED

Two things were measured here, on opposite sides of a boundary that is
not historical.

**In the Rigveda, which can be searched completely.** Seventeen of the
words later Sanskrit uses for writing and sealing are simply not in the
corpus — no *lipi*, no *lekha*, no *mudrā*, no word for ink, pen, tablet,
book or scribe. Nor is there a word for a balance or a pair of scales,
though the corpus does measure: it has a measuring cord, a measuring rod,
a measure of capacity and a measure of distance. A scan of every glossed word in the corpus for the German
and Latin vocabulary of writing returns five words and all five are
accidents of German spelling. The same scan for the vocabulary of sealing
returns nothing at all.

But the corpus is not silent about marking. It has a verb glossed
precisely "to scratch, to incise" — `√rikh-`, twice, where the god Pūṣan
carries an awl and scratches open the hearts of the stingy. It has a
verb for cutting ear-marks into cattle. It has words for a sign, a
banner, a mark, a token. And it is full of the language of obligation:
share, debt, levy, price, measuring.

What it has no word for is **recording** any of it.

**On the Indus side, almost nothing could be reached.** The one
machine-readable corpus available was 179 seals, all carrying CISI
M-numbers — the Mohenjo-daro series, on the digitizer's word, since the
printed corpus could not be opened to check — and all of one kind — the unicorn seals — carrying 984 sign tokens in 181
sign classes, with a median inscription of five signs. That file records
no find-spot, no stratum, no date, no material and no museum number for a
single object.

## WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS

**That the word which later means "written character" meant something
else here.** *Akṣára-* occurs eight times in the Rigveda, and in every
one of the eight it is a syllable of chanted speech or the imperishable:
the syllable that measures the metres, the syllable of the verse in the
highest heaven where the gods are seated. The terminology a reader might
expect to find describing writing is present in the corpus and is doing
different work.

**That "how many Indus signs are there" is a question about a sign
list.** The one dataset reached carries four numbering systems for the
same signs, and inside that file they disagree: 397 classes, 662 Parpola
numbers, 498 Wells numbers, 359 Mahadevan numbers — a factor of 1.8
between the largest and the smallest, with a single class absorbing
seventeen numbers from another system. This is measured on one
annotator's alignment of three published lists, none of which could be
opened here, so as a statement about the published lists it is
provisional and not verified (`DK-M-015`, `DEP-030`). What is not in
doubt is the shape of the problem: whether two shapes are one sign or two
is a decision an analyst makes, and every argument that runs from the
size of the inventory to whether the signs are writing inherits that
decision.

## WHAT COMPLICATES IT

**The measurable half is the textual half, and that is an accident of
access, not of history.** Everything about the Indus material — the
excavation reports, the published corpus, the sign lists, the journal
articles — was unreachable in the session that produced this work.
Everything about the Rigveda was reachable in full, with morphology and
four translations. A record that reports a rich result on one side and an
empty column on the other, and then explains the emptiness historically,
has turned its own limits into a finding. This one does not draw the
comparison at all.

**A liturgical corpus would not mention writing even if its composers had
seen it.** The Rigveda records what the liturgy and the patron needed:
chariots, cattle, soma equipment, the sacrificial ground, the fee. That
is the null explanation of its silence, and it is the explanation the
more interesting ones have to beat.

**The 179 seals are not a sample.** One CISI number-series, one
iconographic class, one annotator, digitised from a printed corpus nobody
here could open. Their
median length of five signs is a measurement of unicorn seals and not of
Indus inscriptions.

## WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN

Whether the Indus signs encode language. When sign use ended. Whether it
continued anywhere after the cities. What the seals were for. Whether
anything was ever written on cloth, leaf or bark. Whether any
Mesopotamian text mentions Meluhhan writing — which is a searchable
question that failed here only on network access, not on evidence.

And one that will not go away by retrieval: even a Rigvedic passage
describing signs cut into stone could not be identified as *Indus*.

## MELAKEELA'S CURRENT INTERPRETATION

That the Rigvedic silence is not evidence of anything yet.

The absence of writing vocabulary in the Rigveda is real, measurable and
measured. What it licenses is a statement about a corpus of praise poetry
— not about what people in the region knew, saw or did. The step from one
to the other needs the Indus side of the evidence, and this record does
not have it.

The one thing the record does say firmly is negative, and it is the thing
§4.K exists to protect: **institutions and archives changing is not
populations disappearing.** If the seals stop and the cities change — and
this record could retrieve no evidence about when either happened — that
still says nothing about who lived there afterwards. People outlive the
institutions that recorded them. That is the ordinary case, and the
inference from one to the other fails on its own terms, before any
evidence is brought to it.

## WHAT WOULD CHANGE IT

- A Rigvedic passage where a marking or incising verb takes a durable
  inscribed surface as its object. None was found in 232 occurrences;
  one would overturn the negative.
- Mahadevan's concordance, or ICIT, read directly — which would replace
  every second-hand corpus figure on this platform with a sourced one.
  MelaKeela's own pages currently give the longest Indus text as ten signs
  on one page and about seventeen on another (`IC-K-001`); neither figure
  has ever been checked here, and this record's 179 seals cannot check
  them.
- Excavation reports with find-spots, which would make the question of
  what the seals were for answerable instead of arguable.
- A Mesopotamian text naming Meluhhan writing or scribes.
- A decipherment that predicts: derived from one set of texts, applied
  to texts it was not built on, read before anyone knows what they say.
