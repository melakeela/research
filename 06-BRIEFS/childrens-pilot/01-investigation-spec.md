# A hundred stone forts — a children's investigation

**Ages:** 8–11.
**Working question, in the child's words:** *Why is a poem not a photograph?*
**Working question, in the institution's words:** what is the relationship
between a textual attestation and a material one, and what does it take to
establish that they refer to the same thing?

**What a child can say at the end.** Three sentences, and they are the exit
criterion for the whole design:

1. **What the poem claims** — "The poem says Indra knocked down a hundred forts
   made of stone. In the whole book, that is the only time it says stone."
2. **What the ground shows** — "We don't know. Nobody in this museum has dug
   anything up for this. That is not the same as nothing being there."
3. **Where the two do not meet** — "You can count words in a poem. You cannot
   count forts in a poem. A photograph would show you one fort. The poem gives
   you a word, and the word is the same word whether there was one fort or none."

If a child leaves with sentences 1 and 3 but not 2, the investigation has
failed, because sentence 2 is the one the museum finds hardest to say.

## 0.1 The four words, and what they mean

The working language is `FOUND / WE THINK / MAYBE / WE DON'T KNOW`
(`01-INHERITED/chatgpt-project-handoff.md` L347;
`06-BACKLOG/EXPANSION-PROMPT-2026-09-07.md` L254 — both
`INHERITED-UNVERIFIED`, and this unit does not promote them; they are a display
vocabulary, not a claim about the past).

The mapping onto the seven statuses of `CLAUDE.md` is fixed, printed on the
child's help screen, and **loses nothing**:

| Register status | Child word | Child gloss shown on screen |
|---|---|---|
| `VERIFIED` | **FOUND** | "Somebody looked it up in a real book and wrote down the page." |
| `PROVISIONAL` | **WE THINK** | "It fits what we found. But only one person has checked, or it is somebody's reading." |
| `HYPOTHESIS` | **MAYBE** | "An idea nobody has tested yet. We are telling you because we don't want to hide it." |
| `HOLD` | **WE DON'T KNOW — we haven't looked yet** | "We know where to look. We have not been able to get there." |
| typed absence (§6) | **WE DON'T KNOW — nobody wrote it down** | "The poem never says. That is not the poem hiding it. It just never says." |
| `REJECTED` / `SUPERSEDED` | **WE GOT THIS WRONG** | "We thought this before. We checked and we were wrong. We keep it here so we don't do it again." |
| `INHERITED-UNVERIFIED` | *(never shown as evidence)* | see §0.2 |

**The split of WE DON'T KNOW into two kinds is load-bearing and is the single
most important thing in this specification.** A child who cannot tell "nobody
wrote it down" from "we haven't looked yet" cannot reach a real *we don't know*,
because the two have different next actions: the first is a fact about the past,
the second is a job on somebody's list. Both are shown with that next action
attached. That is what makes the result a result rather than a consolation.

## 0.2 What may not appear as evidence

- **No `INHERITED-UNVERIFIED` row ever appears on an evidence card.** The
  famous figure "99" reaches this investigation *only* as a `VERIFIED` count of
  six passages (`PUR4J-002`), never as MelaKeela's page headline, which has no
  recorded source. Where the investigation talks about the famous number
  (S-12), it is talking about itself, and it is labelled as that.
- **No reconstruction, no composite, no "typical" fort, no artist's
  impression** (§10.4.4). This investigation contains **no image of a fort at
  all**, and S-10 says so in as many words. A drawing of a fort would answer the
  investigation's question before the child got to it.
- **No people.** See §1 below.

## 1. The prohibition, applied before the design rather than after it

Framework §10.4.7 forbids any interface that asks a child to sort human beings,
under any renaming, and binds **the pattern, not the topic**: `COMPARE` may not
take people, remains, named individuals or populations as its terms *anywhere*.

The fort material sits directly beside that line. `PUR-022` and `PUR4J-030`
record patron-side and opponent-side names — Divodāsa, Ṛjiśvan, Śambara, Pipru,
Śuṣṇa, Purukutsa — and `PUR4J-010` records that the counts sort by narrative
cycle. A `COMPARE` step built on the register's own strongest signal would
therefore hand a child two piles of names and ask which fort belongs to which
side. **That is the forbidden interface**, and framing it as a lesson about how
the poem was later misused would not lift the prohibition, because the child
still performs the sort.

Three design consequences, all binding:

1. **`COMPARE` (S-08) takes exactly four kinds of term: counts, materials,
   grammatical cases, and words.** Nothing else may be loaded into it. The
   interface is built so that a person-card cannot enter it, not so that
   person-cards are filtered out at run time — a subject filter would not
   implement §10.4.7, because the same interaction is the same interaction
   whatever is loaded into it.
2. **Names are read, never operated.** `dívodāsāya` stands in the line the child
   reads at S-01. It stays in the line. It never becomes a card, a bucket, a
   drag target, a filter or an option. A standing card beside it says, in the
   child's words: *"That is a name in the poem. We do not know who it was, or
   whether it was anybody. This museum does not put people into groups, so we
   are not going to ask you to."*
3. **The dāsa / ārya material is out of scope entirely, and the exclusion is
   stated to the child** (§10.4.4's rule for excluded material). `PUR-022`
   records `dāsī-` at 43× its corpus rate inside fort stanzas; that is a real
   measurement and it is not in this investigation. S-10 tells the child: *"There
   are words in these poems about who the fighting was against. We are not
   showing you those here. Grown-ups have used them to sort people into races,
   and we are not going to teach you to do that even to show you it is wrong."*

Human remains do not arise; nothing in the púr- corpus is a remain. The rule
(§10.4.4, D-026) is recorded as not-engaged rather than passed over.

## 2. Posture, mode and route

**Derived posture: Nocturnal Veḷi**, by §1.5 rule 3, *absence dominance*. The
majority of this investigation's load-bearing propositions resolve to typed
absences or holds rather than to positive claims: 82 of 103 passages state no
count (`PUR4J-001`), 93 of 103 state no material (`PUR4J-012`), the geography
field cannot be filled at all (`PUR4J-018`), 48 of 103 poet attributions are
`NOT PRODUCED` (`PUR4J-016`, `HOLD-006`), three of §4J's five types are
`NOT ASSIGNED` (`PUR4J-024`), and there is no ground evidence in the repository
(`HOLD-007`). Rules 1, 2, 4 and 5 do not fire: no withheld access, no repair
state, the substance is Claim Objects rather than Relationship Objects, and the
supporting evidence is textual rather than material or site-bound.

**Assigned posture: Nocturnal Veḷi.** No override; derived and assigned agree.
The `Avoid` for the posture — *"no fantasy portal or occult styling"* — is a
live risk here, because "the mystery of the ninety-nine forts" is exactly the
register the material invites. It is refused: the unknowns in this investigation
are ordinary, countable and specific, and each is shown with what it would take
to close it.

**This is a build gate, not a label.** Under §1.7, Field Mode is *available* in
Nocturnal Veḷi and **forbidden** in Reading Room. Rule 7 of §1.5 makes Reading
Room the residual, and a careless assignment would land there — at which point
this investigation may not be built at all. The posture assignment is therefore
recorded to the Editorial Register (§1.5) with its derivation before any
build begins. Gate G-01 in `04-rules-and-gates.md`.

**Entry route:** the **word route** (§6.4), on `púr-`. Not the object route:
there is no object. Not the place route: there is no place (`PUR4J-018`). This
is itself part of the answer to the investigation's question and is said aloud
at S-02.

### S-00 — Start

**On it.** The question, and an honest description of the shape of the answer.

> **Why is a poem not a photograph?**
>
> About three thousand years ago, somebody made up a poem about knocking down
> forts. We still have the poem. We do not have the forts.
>
> You are going to find out what the poem says, exactly. Then you are going to
> find out what we know about the forts themselves. One of those two jobs goes
> well and one of them does not, and finding out which is which is the point.
>
> Nothing here is a test. Some of the answers are "we don't know", and those
> are real answers, not the ones we give you when we run out.

**Child does.** Nothing. Reads, continues. No name is asked for. No account
exists (§10.4.5).

**Field Bag.** The investigation itself, by identifier and revision (§10.3).

### S-01 — LOOK: one line, three ways

**On it.** One stanza, RV 4.30.20, in the primary-source viewer at full quality
(§7.1, §10.4.3 stage 1): the Devanāgarī (`SRC-084`), the transliterated
Saṃhitā line (`SRC-020`), and the padapāṭha word-split (`SRC-021`). Nothing is
translated yet. Card `EC-01` in `03-evidence-cards.md`.

**Child does.** Three actions, in order.

1. **Counts the words** in the padapāṭha line. There are eight.
2. **Taps any word** to see it highlighted in the other two rows. The word
   `purā́m` is not visibly separable in the Devanāgarī row and *is* separable in
   the padapāṭha row; the interface shows that rather than hiding it.
3. **Answers one question**, multiple choice, no score: *"Is this the piece of
   paper the poet wrote on?"* Options: yes / no / we don't know.

**What they see.** The three rows, and beneath them, three dates: the padapāṭha
word-split is ancient (`PUR-010`, and its note: the analysis is Śākalya's, not a
modern one); the printed Saṃhitā line is Aufrecht's, 1877 (`SRC-020`); the
Devanāgarī setting is Eichler's, modern (`SRC-084`).

**Asked to conclude.**

> **WE DON'T KNOW — we haven't looked yet.** Nobody in this museum has seen the
> first copy of this poem. We do not know that there ever was one written down.
> What we have is a book printed in 1877, which was made from other books.
>
> **FOUND.** Two different people, hundreds of years apart, split this line into
> words. They agree. We checked all 10,552 verses and the split never disagreed
> with the modern one, not once. *(`PUR-010`)*

**Field Bag.** The stanza, by identifier and revision, with its three source
rows.

**Why this screen is first.** The child's first contact with the evidence is
with its *material* condition, not its meaning. A photograph has one date. This
line has at least three, and they are three different kinds of event
(constitution §5 step 2). That is the crack the rest of the investigation opens.

### S-02 — LOOK: one word

**On it.** The word `púr-`, worked as constitution §7 requires — original
script, transliteration, grammatical form, semantic range, context, edition,
locator, translation used, alternatives, and the consequence of choosing between
them — at reading age. Card `EC-02`.

**Child does.** Two actions.

1. **Opens the dictionary entry.** Grassmann, 1873. The gloss is German and is
   shown in German with a plain-English rendering beside it: *Wall aus Steinen
   und Lehm, Verschanzung, Palisade* — "a wall of stones and clay, a bank dug
   for defence, a fence of stakes" (`PUR4J-021`).
2. **Picks a word for it.** The child is offered the words real translators
   used, with who used each and when: *fort* (Griffith 1890, 63 times), *castle*
   (Griffith, 33), *stronghold* (Griffith, 5), *Burg* (Geldner 1951, 79;
   Grassmann, 84), *forteresse* (Renou, 34), *citadelle* (Renou, 22), *city*
   (Griffith, **once** in 103 passages). `PUR4J-022`. The child's pick is
   recorded and is used as the label on every later screen — **their** word, not
   the museum's.

**What they see.** After picking, one line appears:

> **FOUND.** Nobody who has translated this whole book calls it a city — except
> once, in one line out of 103. *(`PUR4J-022`)*
>
> The dictionary says it is a wall of stones and clay. Not a city. Not a castle
> with towers. A wall. *(`PUR4J-021`)*

**Asked to conclude.** One question, free choice, no score: *"You just picked a
word. Did the poem pick it, or did you?"*

**Field Bag.** The word `púr-` as a word-route object (§6.4), with the child's
chosen English label recorded as **theirs** and never mixed with institutional
text on export (§10.4.5, §10.3).

**Why this screen.** This is the translation standard at reading age, and it is
the discipline framework §10.4.2 says the institution most needs to teach. It
also pre-empts the investigation's largest prestige-bias failure mode: a child
who has been handed the English word *fort* for free will spend the rest of the
investigation reasoning about forts. A child who chose it will remember choosing.

### S-03 — ASK

**On it.** The child's own questions, before any more evidence.

**Child does.** Writes up to three questions, or picks from a list of real
`mk:qst:` nodes phrased at reading age (§10.4.3 stage 2). The offered list is
drawn from questions the registers actually carry:

- What was a fort made of?
- How many were there?
- Who lived in them?
- Where were they?
- Did any of this happen?
- Is the poem telling the truth?

**What they see.** Each offered question is marked, before they choose, with
which screen will get to it and which will not:

| Question | What happens to it |
|---|---|
| What was a fort made of? | S-07 answers it. Mostly with "the poem doesn't say." |
| How many were there? | S-04 to S-06. The answer is not a number. |
| Who lived in them? | **Nobody answers it.** `PUR4J-020` — the poem almost never puts anyone inside one. |
| Where were they? | **Nobody answers it.** `PUR4J-018` — we have no map source at all. |
| Did any of this happen? | S-10. The answer is "we don't know", of the second kind. |
| Is the poem telling the truth? | S-12. This one turns out to be the wrong question, and the child is told why. |

**Asked to conclude.** Nothing yet. The child is told, in the child's words,
that two of the six questions on this list have no answer here, and that this
was true before they arrived.

**Field Bag.** The child's questions, marked as theirs (§10.3). **Free text is
local to the device, is never transmitted, and is never published** (§10.4.5).

### S-04 — FIND OUT: does the poem even give a number?

**On it.** The whole púr- corpus as a countable thing: 103 passages
(`PUR-005`), out of 10,552 verses in the book (`PUR-001`).

**Child does.** A tally board, entirely of numbers and passages. 103 cards.
The child sorts them into two piles by reading the label on each: **says a
number** / **says no number**. The cards are pre-labelled — this is a
counting action, not a judgement, and the child is told that the sorting was
done by somebody else and that they can check any card.

**What they see.**

> **FOUND.** 21 of the 103 fort verses give a number. 82 give none.
> *(`PUR4J-001`)*
>
> That means four out of every five times this book mentions a fort, it does not
> say how many.

Then the 21 split into four columns, which the child fills:

| Number the poem says | How many verses |
|---|---|
| a hundred | 9 |
| ninety-nine | 6 |
| seven | 4 |
| ninety | 2 |

*(`PUR4J-002`)*

> **FOUND.** There are only four numbers in the whole book. Never twenty-three,
> never forty-one. Just seven, ninety, ninety-nine and a hundred. *(`PUR4J-009`)*

**Asked to conclude.** One question, three options plus free text, no score:
*"Which is the number of forts in this poem?"* — a hundred / ninety-nine /
there isn't one. The third is the one the evidence supports and it is
**not marked correct**; instead, selecting any option shows the same card:

> **FOUND.** "Ninety-nine" is not even the commonest. A hundred is, by 9 verses
> to 6. *(`PUR4J-003`)*

**Field Bag.** The tally, as a claim set with `PUR4J-001`, `-002`, `-003`,
`-009`, each keeping its `VERIFIED` status (§10.3).

### S-05 — FIND OUT: what "ninety-nine" is made of

**On it.** The six passages that state ninety-nine, in the original, unglossed.
Card `EC-03`.

**Child does.** Reads six short strings and finds two words in each. The task is
literal pattern-matching on transliterated text, with the two target words
highlighted on tap:

| | |
|---|---|
| RV 1.54.6d | puro **navatim** dambhayo **nava** |
| RV 2.19.6c | **navatim** ca **nava** |
| RV 4.26.3b | **nava** sakam **navatih** |
| RV 7.19.5b | **nava** yat puro **navatim** ca |
| RV 7.99.5b | **nava** puro **navatim** ca |
| RV 8.93.2a | **nava** yo **navatim** puro |

*(`PUR4J-004`, quoted exactly as the register's locator carries them —
unaccented. Gate **G-05**: the accented forms come from the pinned corpus
before build; this unit supplies no diacritic of its own.)*

**What they see.**

> **FOUND.** There is no word for ninety-nine. There never is. It is always two
> words: *nava*, nine, and *navatí*, ninety. Sometimes they are next to each
> other and sometimes there are other words in between. *(`PUR4J-004`)*
>
> **FOUND.** And *navatí* is not really a plural. It is one word meaning "a
> ninety" — like saying "a dozen". *(`PUR4J-005`)*

Then, two of the six carry something extra:

> **FOUND.** Twice, right after the ninety-nine, the poem says "and the
> hundredth". But the hundredth thing is not a fort. It is a house, or a place
> to live. *(`PUR4J-008`)*

**Asked to conclude.** *"Ninety-nine and one more makes a hundred. Do you think
the poem is counting, or is it saying 'nearly a hundred'?"* Three options plus
free text: counting / saying nearly a hundred / **we don't know**. All three are
kept. Nothing is marked correct.

**Field Bag.** The six passages, the two-word finding, and the child's answer,
marked as theirs.

### S-06 — FIND OUT: the same words, counting rivers

**On it.** One more line, RV 10.104.8, and it is the hinge of the whole
investigation. Card `EC-04`.

**Child does.** Reads one line, in the same format as the six on S-05, and finds
the same two words. Then taps to see what they are counting.

**What they see.**

> RV 10.104.8c — **navatim** srotya **nava** ca sravantir
>
> Griffith, 1890: *"nine-and-ninety flowing streams"*
> Geldner, 1951: *"die neunundneunzig fließenden Ströme"*
> Grassmann, 1876: *"Die neunundneunzig Flüsse"*
>
> **FOUND.** Exactly the same two words. Not forts. Rivers. *(`PUR4J-006`)*

And, because the register records it and it is the sharper half:

> **FOUND.** That verse also has a fort word in it — one of Indra's names,
> "wall-breaker". So a computer counting words in the same verse would have said
> "ninety-nine forts" here. It would have been wrong. *(`PUR4J-006`)*

**Asked to conclude.** One question, no score, free text plus three options:
*"Does 'nine-and-ninety' mean a number, or does it mean 'a lot'?"* — a number /
a lot / **we don't know**.

Whatever the child answers, the museum's own position appears, labelled:

> **WE THINK.** We think these numbers are a way of talking, not a count of real
> forts somebody remembered. We are not sure. *(`PUR4J-I-01`, PROVISIONAL)*
>
> **And here is what stops us being sure.** The numbers do not get used just
> anywhere. "Seven forts" always turns up in the same story, with the same
> people, and twice in almost exactly the same words. If they were only a way of
> talking, why would they sort themselves out like that? *(`PUR4J-010`,
> PROVISIONAL — and the register demoted this row itself, on review)*

**Field Bag.** The river line, and the child's answer.

**Why this screen exists.** `PUR4J-006` is the corpus's own control case, and it
is the one piece of evidence in the whole register that a child of eight can
verify with their own eyes in under a minute: *same words, different thing.*
It is the answer to "why is a poem not a photograph" in miniature, and it is
placed at the exact centre of the investigation for that reason.

### S-07 — FIND OUT: what were they made of?

**On it.** The material question, and the shape of its answer. Card `EC-05`.

**Child does.** A materials shelf. 103 cards face-down, one per passage. The
child turns over any ten, twenty or all of them. Ten of the 103 have a material
written on them. Ninety-three are blank.

The interface does **not** shuffle to put the ten early. A child who turns over
twelve cards will most likely see none of them, and that is the lesson.

**What they see.** After the child has turned over at least twelve cards, a
counter appears and stays:

| | |
|---|---|
| made of metal | 8 passages |
| made of stone | **1 passage** |
| raw, not baked | 1 passage |
| doesn't say | 93 passages |

*(`PUR4J-012`)*

Then the stone one, which is the stanza from S-01, now translated:

> **RV 4.30.20** — *śatám aśmanmáyīnām purā́m índro vy āā̀syat / dívodāsāya
> dāśúṣe*
>
> *(quoted exactly as `PUR-P-041` stores it. `vy āā̀syat` is an encoding
> artefact of the register file, not a reading; `PUR4J-014`'s locator gives
> `vy asyat`. Gate **G-05** resolves it against the pinned corpus. It is not
> silently repaired here, because silently repairing a quotation is the move
> this whole investigation is about.)*
>
> "Indra threw down a hundred stone forts, for Divodāsa who gave offerings."
> *(`PUR4J-014`; and see gate G-05 — the translators' own words at this verse
> must be pulled before this screen is built, and the museum's paraphrase
> replaced with them)*
>
> **FOUND.** This is the only time in the whole book a fort is made of stone.
> *(`PUR4J-012`)*

And the metal, which does not settle:

> **WE DON'T KNOW — grown-ups disagree.** Eight forts are made of a metal. One
> translator says iron, every time. Another says bronze, every time. Neither of
> them says why. Iron and bronze come from different times, so this changes how
> old the poem might be. *(`PUR4J-023`)*

Then the typed absence, in the child's words:

> **WE DON'T KNOW — nobody wrote it down.** 93 verses never say what the fort
> was made of. That is not the poem hiding it from us. A song about knocking a
> wall down had no reason to say what the wall was made of. *(`PUR4J-012`,
> typed `NOT PRODUCED`)*

**Asked to conclude.** *"If you only had this poem, could you tell somebody what
these forts looked like?"* Options: yes / a bit / no. All kept, none correct.

**Field Bag.** The materials count, the stone verse, the iron/bronze
disagreement.

### S-08 — COMPARE

**On it.** The only comparison step. Its terms are **counts, materials,
grammatical cases and words**. See §1: no person, name, group or population may
be loaded into this interface, and it is built so that none can be.

**Child does.** Three comparisons, side by side, each two cards.

1. **Stone against raw.** The one stone fort (RV 4.30.20) beside the one
   unbaked-earth fort (RV 2.35.6). What is alike: both are single, both are
   materials. What differs: one is being knocked down, one is somewhere safe.
2. **A hundred against ninety-nine.** Nine verses beside six. What is alike:
   both are round-ish, both attach to the same stories. What differs: one is a
   single word, one is never a single word.
3. **A thing knocked down against a place you are in.** Two grammar cards. Of
   the 83 plain fort-words in the book, 56 are the thing a verb is done *to*,
   and **3** say somebody is *in* one. `PUR4J-020`.

**What they see.** After comparison 3:

> **FOUND.** Nearly seven times out of ten, a fort in this poem is a thing being
> broken. Three times out of eighty-three, it is a place somebody is inside.
> *(`PUR4J-020`)*
>
> **MAYBE.** So maybe these were somebody else's forts, and the poem is only
> ever on the outside of them. Nobody has tested that properly yet.
> *(`PUR-027`, HYPOTHESIS)*

Beside the stone/raw comparison, the museum's caution, in the child's words:

> **WE THINK.** The stone one belongs to the other side. The raw one is a safe
> place. But there is only one of each, and you cannot see a pattern in one and
> one. *(`PUR4J-014`, PROVISIONAL — and the register says exactly this: "with
> n=1 on each side no distribution follows")*

**Asked to conclude.** *"Two things can look the same and be different. Show one
pair from today where that happened."* The child picks a pair they already saw.
The expected pick is S-06's rivers, and it is not required.

**Field Bag.** The three comparisons as Relationship Objects, each with its
status.

**Standing card on this screen, always visible:**

> There are names of people in these poems. We are not going to ask you to sort
> them, or guess which group anybody belonged to. Grown-ups have done that with
> these poems and got it badly wrong. Forts and pots you can put side by side.
> People you cannot. *(§10.4.4, §10.4.7)*

### S-09 — FIND OUT: when in the poem? *(the MAYBE screen)*

**On it.** The stratum evidence, presented entirely under MAYBE, because the
whole screen rests on a `HYPOTHESIS`.

**A banner sits above this screen and cannot be dismissed:**

> **Everything on this page is a MAYBE.** It all depends on one man's idea, from
> 1905, about which bits of the book are older. That idea has never been checked
> against a second, separate way of telling. We are showing you because it is
> interesting and because hiding it would be worse. *(`PUR-028`, HYPOTHESIS;
> `PUR-013`, single source)*

**Child does.** Places 106 counters into five bins labelled by Arnold's period
names, then presses one button: **"show what you'd expect"**, which overlays the
whole book's shares.

**What they see.**

| Period | Fort words found | If forts were spread evenly |
|---|---:|---:|
| Archaic | 31 | 21.8 |
| Strophic | 32 | 24.0 |
| Normal | 17 | 26.3 |
| Cretic | 23 | 19.1 |
| Popular | **3** | **14.8** |

*(`PUR-014`, `PUR-016`, `PUR-017`)*

> **FOUND.** In the part he called the newest, we expected about 15 fort words
> and found 3. *(`PUR-017`)*
>
> **MAYBE.** So maybe fort-talk is an old part of this poem, not something added
> late. *(`PUR-026`, PROVISIONAL — and it rests on `PUR-028`, a MAYBE, so it
> cannot be more than a WE THINK)*

And immediately, because the register insists on it:

> **We checked a second way and it did not agree.** Sorting the poems by which
> book they are in gives no difference at all. *(`PUR-020`)*
>
> **And the two ways are not really separate anyway.** Most of the "newest"
> verses are in one book, so we partly counted the same thing twice.
> *(`PUR-021`)*

**Asked to conclude.** *"One test says yes. One test says nothing. And the two
tests are partly the same test. What would you do?"* Options: believe the first
one / believe neither / **find a third test**. All kept.

**Field Bag.** The distribution, with `PUR-028` attached to it as the thing it
stands on.

**Why this screen is in.** It is the only screen where a child meets a real
statistical result *and* the reason not to trust it yet, in the same place. That
is what a MAYBE is for, and an investigation with no MAYBE screen teaches that
the four words are three words.

### S-10 — THE GROUND

**On it.** The empty box. This is the screen the whole investigation is for.

**Child does.** One action: opens the museum's own list of everything it has
looked at, and searches it. The list is real — all 88 rows of
`02-SOURCES/access-ledger.csv`, at reading age, showing what each one is. The
child is invited to look for anything that came out of the ground.

There is nothing to find. The search returns empty, and the interface says so
plainly rather than showing a spinner or a "no results, try again".

**What they see.**

> **WE DON'T KNOW — we haven't looked yet.**
>
> We have 88 things on our list. Poems, dictionaries, translations, a book about
> poetry rhythms from 1905. Not one of them is a dig. Nobody in this museum has
> looked at a wall, a brick, a post-hole, a ditch or a broken pot for this
> investigation.
>
> So when you ask "was there really a fort", the honest answer is: **we have not
> looked.** That is different from "there wasn't one." *(`HOLD-007`)*

Then, the second kind, which is not fixable by looking:

> **WE DON'T KNOW — nobody wrote it down.**
>
> The poem never says where. Not once, in any of the 103 verses. There is one
> word that might be a river's name or might just mean "a river", and two
> translators read it differently. *(`PUR4J-018`, `PUR4J-028`)*
>
> So even if somebody dug up a wall tomorrow, we would have a wall and a poem
> and no way to say they were the same wall.

Then the one thing the poem might have named, and did not quite:

> **WE DON'T KNOW — grown-ups disagree.** One fort in the whole book might have
> a name. One translator reads *narmini* as a name — "the fort Narmini". The
> other reads the same word as "joyous". The dictionary refuses to choose. If
> the first is right, that is the only named fort in 103 verses. If the second
> is right, there are none. *(`PUR4J-015`)*

And the exclusion, stated as a decision the museum made (§10.4.4):

> There are words in these poems about who the fighting was against. We are not
> showing you those here. Grown-ups have used them to sort people into races,
> and we are not going to teach you to do that even to show you it is wrong.
> When you are older you can read about it, with the dates and who said it.
> *(§10.4.7)*

**Asked to conclude.** The screen's one question, and the only place in the
investigation where "we don't know" is offered as the **first** option rather
than the last:

> *"Was there really a stone fort?"*
>
> - **We don't know, and nobody here has looked.**
> - We don't know, and even if we looked we might not be able to tell.
> - Yes.
> - No.

Selecting either of the first two shows: **"That is the same thing the museum
says."** Selecting yes or no shows: **"That might turn out to be right. Right
now nobody here can show you why."** No option is marked correct or incorrect.

**Field Bag.** `HOLD-007`, in full, with what it would take to close it. A child
leaves holding the museum's own to-do list.

**Design note.** There is no image on this screen and there is no image of a
fort anywhere in the investigation. An illustration here would be a
reconstruction of the exact thing the screen says we do not have (§10.4.4).

### S-11 — DECIDE

**On it.** Six statements, and four bins. The child sorts *statements*, not
things and not people.

**Child does.** Drags each statement into `FOUND` / `WE THINK` / `MAYBE` /
`WE DON'T KNOW`. There is no correct arrangement shown, no score, no timer.

| Statement | Where the museum puts it |
|---|---|
| "The poem says a hundred stone forts." | FOUND (`PUR4J-012`, `PUR4J-002`) |
| "Only one verse in the whole book says stone." | FOUND (`PUR4J-012`) |
| "Ninety-nine is the famous number." | FOUND — but as a fact about *people today*, not about the poem (`PUR4J-I-02`) |
| "Ninety-nine is the poem's commonest number." | **Not true.** A hundred is. (`PUR4J-003`) |
| "The forts were made of iron." | WE DON'T KNOW — translators disagree (`PUR4J-023`) |
| "There was a stone fort somewhere." | WE DON'T KNOW — we haven't looked (`HOLD-007`) |

The fourth statement is the only one in the investigation the child can get
*wrong against the evidence*, and the interface still does not mark it. It shows
the count again and lets them move it or not.

**Then the investigation's question**, in the child's words, free text plus four
options:

> **Why is a poem not a photograph?**
>
> - A photograph shows one thing. A poem uses the same words for lots of things.
> - A photograph has a place. This poem never says where.
> - A photograph is made once. This poem was copied and copied.
> - **We don't know / something else** *(free text)*

All four are supportable from what the child saw, and the interface says so.
The child may pick more than one.

**Field Bag.** The child's six placements and their answer, all marked as theirs
and never mixed with institutional text (§10.4.5).

### S-12 — CHECK: what the museum thinks, and where it has been wrong

**On it.** The institution's own position, disclosed **last** (§9.3), with
status, and with the places it changed its mind.

**Child does.** Reads. Then one action: taps any place where their answer
differed from the museum's, and is shown that difference as *interesting*
(§10.4.3 stage 5).

**What they see.** The museum's position, in the constitution's step-14 shape at
reading age:

> **What we think.** These numbers are probably a way of talking, not a count.
> **WE THINK**, not FOUND. *(`PUR4J-I-01`, PROVISIONAL)*
>
> **What makes it complicated.** The numbers sort themselves out by story, and a
> way of talking should not do that. *(`PUR4J-010`, PROVISIONAL)*
>
> **What we don't know.** Whether any of these forts were real places. We have
> not looked. *(`HOLD-007`)*
>
> **What would change our mind.** If somebody found a verse with an odd number
> in it — twenty-three forts, forty-one forts — the "way of talking" idea would
> be in trouble. There isn't one, so far. *(`PUR4J-009`, `PUR4J-I-01` falsifier)*

Then, the "we got this wrong" cards, which are the reason for this screen:

> **WE GOT THIS WRONG.** We first read one verse as "hundreds of forts". We went
> back and looked properly, and it says "a hundred", the same as another verse
> we had already read that way. Three translators all agree. We were wrong and
> we changed it. *(`PUR4J-002`, recorded correction)*
>
> **WE GOT THIS WRONG.** We sorted all 103 verses into "a real fort in the
> story" and "just a way of speaking", and we got 11 of them the wrong way
> round. We found it by testing our own rule against itself. Fixing it did not
> change the answer, and we are telling you anyway. *(`PUR4J-029`)*
>
> **WE GOT THIS WRONG.** We said one thing was a solid finding when it was
> really just somebody's reading. Somebody checking our work spotted it and we
> moved it down from FOUND to WE THINK. *(`PUR4J-010`, `PUR4J-014` — both
> demoted on review)*

And the answer to S-03's sixth question, which the child was told was the wrong
question:

> You asked whether the poem is telling the truth. That turns out to be the
> wrong question, and here is why.
>
> A poem is not trying to be a photograph. It was made to be sung, about
> somebody winning. Asking whether it is lying is like asking whether a song
> about your team winning is lying. What we can ask is: **what does it say,
> exactly, and what would we need to find out whether anything like that
> happened?** That is what you just did.

**Asked to conclude.** *"Did you agree with us? Where didn't you?"* A
disagreement is recorded with which screen and which evidence drove it, and is
presented as interesting, not wrong (§10.4.3 stage 5).

**Where a disagreement goes.** Nowhere, by default. Framework §9.4 routes a
PROVE IT disagreement into the correction pipeline as a challenge candidate;
§10.4.5 forbids a child's free text being transmitted or published. **§10.4.5
governs.** A child's disagreement stays on the device. A classroom may submit
one, by the teacher, from the teacher's account, as the class's (§10.4.5, §9.4)
— and classroom mode is not specified here (§4 below).

### S-13 — FIELD BAG

**On it.** What the child collected, and what it is for.

**Child does.** Reviews the bag. Exports it if they want to (§5.2): a dated,
revision-pinned, citable set. Nothing is uploaded. The bag is local and no
account exists (§10.3, §10.4.5).

**What they see.** Every item with the status it was collected under — a MAYBE
collected on S-09 still reads MAYBE (§10.3). And one line about time:

> If you come back, this bag will tell you what changed. If we found something
> new, or found out we were wrong, it will say so.

**Asked to conclude.** Nothing. The investigation ends without a verdict,
because it does not have one.

**Closing card:**

> **What you can say now:**
>
> The poem says Indra knocked down a hundred stone forts. It says "stone" once
> in the whole book, and it never says where.
>
> The ground has not been looked at. Not by us. That is a job, not an answer.
>
> A poem is not a photograph, and the reason is not that poems lie. It is that a
> poem uses the same words for a fort and for a river, and never tells you which
> one it means.

## 4. What is deliberately not specified here

- **Classroom mode** (§10.5). It is a separate layer over the same objects and
  it needs the teacher-preparation and rights-clearance work of §10.5.1 per
  item. Not started. This investigation is buildable without it and is worse
  without it.
- **Any second investigation.** This is one, complete.
- **Visual design, layout, typography, interaction timing, accessibility
  implementation.** Out of scope for a specification unit and out of scope for
  this repository (reconciliation C-9: registers and briefs, never pages).
- **The `mk:qst:` identifiers** for S-03's question list. The identifier scheme
  is framework §2.1 and no `mk:` identifier has been minted in this repository;
  the questions are specified by their text and must be bound to real nodes
  before build (gate G-07).

## 5. What would change this specification

- **Retrieval of any archaeological source bearing on the púr- corpus** would
  turn S-10 from an empty box into a comparison — and would then need §10.4.4's
  bridge discipline, because a wall and a poem are two claims, not one. The
  investigation would get harder, not easier, and the screen would grow rather
  than shrink.
- **A second, independent stratification instrument** would move `PUR-028` off
  `HYPOTHESIS`, and S-09 would stop being a MAYBE screen. The investigation
  would lose its clearest teaching case for what a MAYBE is; a replacement would
  be needed.
- **Resolution of `HOLD-001`** (the `púraṃdhi-` etymology) changes the corpus
  from 106 tokens to 156 (`PUR-023`) and every count on S-04 and S-09 with it.
- **An owner ruling on D-045** (whether the site-facing artefact keeps the name
  "The 99 Forts Database") does not change any screen, because no screen uses
  the inherited number as a quantity. That is by design and is checked at gate
  G-04.
