# Domain K — translation blocks and the English-category audit

Constitution §7 requires, for every consequential ancient word: original
script where available, transliteration, grammatical form, semantic range,
textual context, edition, exact locator, the translation used, the
alternatives, and the interpretive consequence of choosing between them.
Three words in this unit are consequential enough to carry the whole block,
and five English words are consequential enough to be audited before use.

Sources throughout: VedaWeb `d3eb8af` — Eichler Devanāgarī (`SRC-084`),
Lubotsky padapāṭha (`SRC-021`), Zurich morphology (`SRC-022`), Grassmann
glosses via `info/matched_lemmata.json`, Griffith 1890 (`SRC-073`),
Geldner 1951 (`SRC-072`). Retrieved 2026-09-09 under `SRC-106`.

## 1. `akṣára-`

**Why it is consequential.** In Classical Sanskrit *akṣara-* is the ordinary
word for a written character; a reader who carries that sense backwards finds
the Rigveda talking about letters eight times. It is also the word an argument
for writing in the Rigvedic period would most want to find.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Script** | अ॒क्षरे॑ (RV 1.164.39a), अ॒क्षरे॑ण (1.164.24d), अ॒क्षरे॑ण (10.13.3c) |
| **Transliteration** | `akṣáre` LOC.SG.N; `akṣáreṇa` INS.SG.N; also `akṣáram` NOM.SG.N, `akṣárā` |
| **Grammatical form** | neuter *a*-stem noun; one feminine `akṣárā` at 1.34.4d and 7.1.14c |
| **Padapāṭha** | `r̥caḥ | akṣare | parame | vi-oman | yasmin | devāḥ | adhi | viśve | ni-seduḥ` (1.164.39a–b) |
| **Semantic range (Grassmann)** | "unvergänglich; Silbe" — imperishable; syllable |
| **Edition** | Aufrecht / Lubotsky text as pinned in VedaWeb `d3eb8af` |
| **Locators** | 1.34.4d, 1.164.24d, 1.164.39a, 1.164.42c, 3.55.1b, 6.16.35b, 7.1.14c, 10.13.3c — `DK-OCC-222` to `DK-OCC-229` in `03-REGISTERS/domain-k-rigveda-marking-occurrences.csv` |
| **Translation used** | none. The register's `sense` column reads NOT ASSIGNED. |
| **Alternatives shown** | Griffith 1890 "syllable" (1.164.24, 1.164.39, 10.13.3), "imperishable" (1.164.42, 7.1.14); Geldner 1951 "Silbe", "das Unvergängliche", "das große Wort" (3.55.1), "Rede(?)" (6.16.35) |
| **Interpretive consequence** | If *akṣára-* is read as "written character", RV 1.164.39 becomes a statement about a script and the domain's central absence disappears. If it is read as "syllable", the same verse is about the chanted *r̥c* and the absence stands. Both translators take the second reading in every one of the eight passages, and the verse itself supplies the context: 1.164.24 measures metres *by* the *akṣára-*, and 1.164.39 places it in the highest heaven where the gods sit — the unit of an utterance, not a mark on a surface. |

**The reading this record adopts, and what would change it.** None is adopted:
`DK-R-008` records what the word does in the eight passages and stops there.
What would change the picture is a Rigvedic passage where *akṣára-* stands
with a verb of making, an instrument, or a surface. There is none in the
corpus; that is the measurement, and it is falsifiable by anyone who finds one.

## 2. `√rikh-` / `√likh-`

| | |
|---|---|
| **Script** | ...त॒या स॑म॒स्य हृद॑य॒म् आ रि॑ख किकि॒रा कृ॑णु (RV 6.53.8c–d) |
| **Transliteration** | `ā́ rikha` — 2SG.IMP.ACT of `√rikh-`, with preverb `ā́` |
| **Padapāṭha** | `tayā | samasya | hr̥dayam | ā | rikha | kikirā | kr̥ṇu` |
| **Semantic range (Grassmann)** | "ritzen" — to scratch, to incise |
| **Textual context** | Pūṣan, addressed as `āghr̥ṇe`, carries an `ā́rā` — an awl or goad — described as `brahmacódanī-`, "urging the solemn speech"; with it he is to scratch open the heart of everyone. The preceding stanza, 6.53.7, applies the same verb to "the hearts of the stingy". |
| **Locators** | 6.53.7a, 6.53.8d — `DK-OCC-039`, `DK-OCC-040` |
| **Translation used** | none assigned |
| **Alternatives shown** | Griffith "Tear up and read in pieces… the hearts"; Geldner "Ritze, kratze(?) die Herzen … auf" — Geldner's own question mark retained |
| **Interpretive consequence** | `√rikh-` and Classical `√likh-` "scratch, write" belong to one root family. Its presence in the Rigveda means the statement "the root that later means *write* is absent from the Rigveda" is **false**, and a candidate list built from Classical Sanskrit produces exactly that false statement. What is absent is not the verb but its application: the Rigvedic incising is done with an awl, to a heart, by a god. Whether the Classical semantic development ran through "scratch a surface" cannot be tested here — every source that would date and evidence it is blocked (`SRC-109` to `SRC-114`), and that is `HOLD-008`. |

## 3. `várṇa-`

Carried, not re-derived: 23 occurrences, all in
`03-REGISTERS/rigveda-varna-occurrences.csv`, measured by an earlier unit
(`VAR-001`). Grassmann's semantic range is "Farbe, Stamm, Art, Gattung,
Partei, Menschenart, Stand, Kaste". The later sense *letter of the alphabet*
is not among the senses he assigns anywhere in this corpus. The
interpretive consequence for domain K is small and worth stating anyway:
*várṇa-* is the second word such an argument would reach
for, and in this corpus it is not doing that work either. Its much larger
consequence — the *colour → class → caste* chain — belongs to domains I
and V and is not touched here.

## 4. The English-category audit

§7 names *race, tribe, slave, barbarian, fort, religion, caste,
civilization, invasion, indigenous* as inherited English categories to audit
before use. Domain K needs five more, and each one, used unaudited, decides
the question before the evidence is in.

**"Script."** In English the word already asserts that the signs encode
language. The Indus material is a corpus of signs whose linguistic status is
exactly what is disputed. This record therefore says *sign system*, *signs*,
*sign classes* for the object, and reserves *script* for the disputed claim
itself — which is registered as a hypothesis (`DK-H-001`), not as a
description. Where earlier repository text or a source says "the Indus
script", that is the source's word, quoted.

**"Writing."** Same problem, one step further: *writing* imports both
language-encoding and a reading community. `DK-R-003` measures the absence of
a *writing* vocabulary in the Rigveda; it does not thereby establish that the
Indus signs were writing that the Rigvedic world failed to describe.

**"Administrators."** The task that commissioned this unit forbade
*Harappan administrators*, and rightly: the word presumes an office, a
hierarchy and a delegated authority, none of which is evidenced by a seal.
This record says **sign-makers** and **sign-users**, with institutional role
unresolved, and where a function is proposed it is registered as a
hypothesis with its own evidence line (`DK-H-003`, `DK-H-004`).

**"Archive."** An archive is a deliberate, curated accumulation kept for
later consultation. A deposit of sealings in a room is not an archive until
someone shows the keeping and the consulting. This record uses *deposit*,
*assemblage*, *find-group* for what is excavated and *archive* only inside a
claim that has been gated. The same discipline applies in the other
direction: the Rigveda is called a *curated oral corpus*, not an archive of
its society.

**"Literacy" and "illiterate."** Both words rank societies on a scale whose
top is the modern school. Neither is used of a society in this record's own
voice — not "a literate Harappan civilization", not "an illiterate Vedic
one" — and adversarial review was right that the first version of this rule,
"neither appears in this record's own voice", was contradicted two pages
earlier by "an argument for Indus literacy". That phrase is now gone and the
rule is stated at the width it is actually kept. The
questions they usually smuggle — how many people could read, what they could
read, and what a person did who could not — are separate empirical questions,
and none of them is answerable from anything retrieved here.

**"Decipherment."** Used only of a proposal, never of an outcome, and always
with the proposer named. `SRC-104` claims one; it is registered at
`DK-M-021` as a claim its own repository makes and gated at `DK-H-007`.
