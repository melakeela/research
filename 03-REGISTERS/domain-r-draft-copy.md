## THE BLUE ROAD

### QUESTION

Where did the lapis lazuli in the ancient world come from, and how do we know?

### WHAT IS OBSERVED

Objects of a deep blue stone turn up a very long way from anywhere it forms — in
Mesopotamian burials, at Ebla in Syria, at Amarna in Egypt, in Achaemenid
Persepolis, and on the painted walls of caves in the Indian Deccan. In the
cuneiform record the word usually translated "lapis" appears in 354 texts, and
the earliest of them, by the tablets' own cataloguing, is from Ebla around
2350 BCE; twenty of them are from Amarna (`DRB-018`).

The stone is rare: the published literature names about eleven deposits in the
world (`DRB-001`).

### WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS

That the stone moved very long distances, early, and continuously. That is the
one inference the method actually licenses, and it has been the same inference
since Damour stated it in 1865: an object of foreign material implies that the
object, or the matter it is made of, was transported (`DRM-001`).

That Badakhshan in Afghanistan — the Sar-e Sang mines — is the best-supported
single source. **Provisionally**: the strongest published result rests on one
marker, the nickel-to-copper ratio in the pyrite grains inside the blue rock,
in a single study whose supporting data this record has not seen (`DRB-003`,
`DRB-010`, which is `PROVISIONAL`).

### WHAT COMPLICATES IT

Four things, and together they are most of what there is to say.

**The alternatives were mostly never tested.** Seven of the eleven deposits are
treated as candidate ancient sources, and the most recent published protocol
compares objects against **four** (`DRB-001`, `DRB-002`). This matters because
of how such a comparison works: the statistical method assigns an object to the
group it most resembles **even when the group it actually belongs to is not in
the dataset** (`DRM-002`). A four-source protocol cannot return "none of these."

**More sampling would probably blur the picture, not sharpen it.** The authors
of one reference dataset expected that adding geological samples would produce
*more* overlap between sources, not less (`DRM-003`). Clean separation can be a
symptom of thin sampling.

**The main rival source was removed by reassignment.** The Chagai Hills in
Pakistan were the leading alternative for the eastern material. The deposit's
existence is now doubted — "neither documented nor supported by geological
data" — and the rock samples associated with it are held to have come from
Badakhshan (`DRB-005`). Once that is accepted, Badakhshan becomes the most
probable source (`DRB-006`). The reasoning has a stated basis and may well be
right. It is worth seeing plainly all the same: the alternative was eliminated
by reattributing its reference material to the favoured answer.

**And the instruments do not always agree with themselves.** In the Persepolis
study the objects were powdered and measured one way, the reference rocks
measured whole another way, and the authors attribute the divergence between
them to that difference rather than to source (`DRB-007`). A later paper judges
that study's mineralogy "not very informative for provenance", because the
Asian sources do not differ much mineralogically (`DRB-008`). At Konar Sandal in
Iran the same kind of cross-technique comparison produced results the analysts
called inconclusive (`DRB-009`).

### WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN

Who mined it. Who carried it, and in how many stages. Whether any given object
crossed the Hindu Kush or came from somewhere closer that nobody has surveyed.

And one whole site: **Shortugai**, in Badakhshan, where an Indus-related
settlement is said to sit beside the mines. This record could not reach a single
publication about it and therefore says nothing about it at all — not that it
was an Indus outpost, and not that it was not (`HOLD-008`, `DR-NEG-002`).

The texts do not close the gap either, and one of them opens it. Darius's
foundation charter at Susa names **Sogdiana** as the place lapis was brought
from — and Sar-e Sang is not in Sogdiana (`DRB-013`). The inscription records a
supplier, not a mine, and those are different questions.

### MELAKEELA'S CURRENT INTERPRETATION

Badakhshan is the leading hypothesis and is held as one. It is not treated as
the default answer, because the alternatives were not excluded — most were never
sampled — and because the one that was seriously entertained was removed by
reassigning its evidence.

**Not every blue object is Badakhshan lapis, and not every blue object is
lapis.** Both halves are literal. In the largest reachable object catalogue,
285 objects are attributed to lapis lazuli and 86 to carnelian; 202 more are
glass, faience, Egyptian blue, frit, azurite, turquoise, sodalite or jasper —
materials confusable with those two and mineralogically distinct from both — and
**none of the 796 has an analysis on record** (`DRM-021`, `DR-NEG-003`). At
Konar Sandal, beads identified as calcite by eye turned out on analysis to be
gypsum alabaster (`DRM-005`).

### WHAT WOULD CHANGE IT

- A survey of the Chagai district, either finding a lazurite deposit or
  establishing that there is none. A field question with a determinate answer,
  and the cheapest decisive test on this road (`HYP-R-002`).
- A well-sampled study, against a reference set including the sources nobody
  samples, assigning a substantial share of any assemblage away from Badakhshan.
- **Analysis of the sixteen lapis beads on the Kish necklace whose fifteen
  carnelian beads have already been analysed** (`DR-NEG-009`). One necklace, one
  museum, one instrument, and both roads measured in a single burial.

## THE RED ROAD

### QUESTION

Who made the carnelian beads found in Mesopotamian graves, and where did the
stone come from?

### WHAT IS OBSERVED

Long orange beads, some with white designs bleached into them, in third-millennium
Mesopotamian burials — including the Early Dynastic III royal graves at Ur
(`DRR-016`) and a necklace from a burial at Kish (`DRR-001`). The Mesopotamian
plain has no agate or carnelian at all, so every one of them was imported —
from Iran, Turkey, Arabia or the Indus region (`DRR-010`).

### WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS

For one well-studied necklace from Kish, three separate things, and they do not
all point the same way.

**The stone is Indian.** The study reports that all fifteen carnelian beads
assign to deposits in the Deccan Traps, and within them to Gujarat (`DRR-001`;
the object dossier `OBJ-R-001` is `PROVISIONAL`, because the per-bead table was
not delivered and no second laboratory can presently check the result).

**The technique is Indus.** All fifteen were drilled with a distinctive
constricted cylindrical bit, made of a stone called Ernestite, which is an Indus
technology (`DRR-007`).

**The workmanship is not all Indus.** Four beads on that same string have ends
chipped and only partly smoothed rather than ground flat as Indus finishing
requires, and the white designs on two of them are less refined than those on
their neighbours (`DRR-007`).

One necklace, therefore, separates four things that are usually run together:
where the stone formed, where it was worked, in whose tradition, and to whose
standard.

### WHAT COMPLICATES IT

**"Gujarat" is as precise as the method gets.** The reference database
misclassifies about three geological samples in ten at the level of an
individual deposit, and correctly places about nineteen in twenty at the level
of a region (`DRR-002`). Narrowing the comparison to ten Deccan sources did not
improve it (`DRR-003`). So a named quarry should be read as a named region.

**No one else can check it.** Carnelian analyses are not currently comparable
between laboratories (`DRR-015`). This result has no independent replication and
cannot presently have one.

**The Arabian alternative is the weakest-sampled part of the comparison.** Some
sources in the database are stand-ins: archaeological debris used for a nearby
deposit, and samples bought from stone merchants (`DRR-004`). Those stand-ins
are the Arabian and Anatolian entries — the alternatives to India for a
Mesopotamian bead.

**And an Arabian source does exist.** At Jebel al-Ma'taradh in Ra's al-Khaimah,
in the region usually identified as Magan, deposits of carnelian, agate,
chalcedony and chrysoprase have been mapped — twenty-six veins (`OBJ-R-013`) —
and worked from at least the seventh millennium BCE. Before that survey it was
held that *no* such source existed on the Arabian coast of the Gulf (`DRR-005`).

That is worth stopping on. Every carnelian bead in the Gulf that was attributed
to India **because there was no local source** was attributed on a premise that
has since failed. It does not follow that those attributions are wrong. It
follows that their reason is gone.

### WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN

Whether Indus people lived in Mesopotamian cities and made these beads there, or
whether the tools and the technique travelled without them. The study's own
phrasing — that Indus craftspeople produced beads "or taught others to make"
them — concedes the second possibility, and drill-hole shape cannot decide
between them (`DRR-008`, `BR-R-002`).

What went the other way. Indus goods travel west in quantity; there is a "lack
of substantive evidence for reciprocal exchange" going east (`DRR-016`). But the
things that would show a return flow — textiles, wool, grain, oil, silver — are
exactly the things that do not survive or are melted down (`DR-NEG-005`). The
asymmetry may be in the durability, not the trade.

And the makers. Not one bead-maker, driller, miner or carrier is named in any
source used here, on either road, in any period (`APA-R-007`).

### MELAKEELA'S CURRENT INTERPRETATION

The Indus technological repertoire was operating in Mesopotamia. That much the
evidence carries. Whose hands held it is a further claim and is held open.

The stone in the Kish necklace is, on the one published analysis, Gujarati at
the resolution the method supports — a regional statement, held provisionally
(`DRR-001`, `DRR-002`, `OBJ-R-001`). Gulf finds are a separate question, and one
that cannot be settled
until the Arabian source is in the reference database — because what the method
returns depends on what was put in it.

### WHAT WOULD CHANGE IT

- Field-collected Jebel al-Ma'taradh samples added to the carnelian reference
  database, and Gulf beads re-run against it (`HYP-R-005`). Decisive either way.
- Ernestite drill bits found in Mesopotamian contexts **without** production
  debris, which would show the tools travelling as goods.
- **The unfinished beads Woolley excavated at Ur, which have never been studied
  in detail** (`DR-NEG-010`). They are the evidence that would settle a
  disagreement between two excavators from the 1920s, and they have been in
  museum drawers for ninety years.

## A NOTE ON THE FOUR NAMES

Dilmun, Magan, Meluhha and Marhaši are Mesopotamian words. They are what one
society called four others, and there is no surviving text from any of the four
naming itself. Of the 791 texts, 720 have a recorded findspot and 716 of those
are in Mesopotamia, Syria or Assyria; the four exceptions are three Old Akkadian
texts from Susa and one from Alalakh, and none is from the Indus region,
Gujarat, Oman or Bahrain (`DRM-010`).

They are usually introduced as a set. In the surviving record they are almost
never a set: of 791 texts naming at least one, **742 name exactly one**, and all
four appear together in a single text (`DRM-006`). The grouping comes from a
small number of royal and literary texts, not from the administrative mass.

Nor is one name one kind of thing. `me-luh-ha` is written with the determinative
for a land, and for a place, and for a **bird**, and it qualifies two kinds of
**timber**, and it stands in personal names. The same is true of the others once
you look: 115 occurrences of `ma2-gan` are marked as **timber**, four of
`dilmun` as **bronze**, and one of `mar-ha-szi` as a **vegetable** — an onion
(`DRM-007`). And the marking does not stay put: in the third millennium and the
Old Babylonian period `me-luh-ha` takes the place-marker, in texts of mixed
kinds at Girsu, Ur and Nippur; more than a thousand years later it takes the
land-marker, sixty-four times at Nineveh, in Assyrian kings' accounts of where
they marched (`DRM-008`). Whatever it meant, it did not go on meaning the same
thing.

**Material movement, population movement and language movement are three
claims.** In this record the first is often established, the second is sometimes
arguable, and the third is not in evidence at all. The clearest case: men
recorded in the Girsu province as *dumu me-luh-ha*, "son of Meluhha", bear
Sumerian names built on Sumerian gods — Ur-Lamma, Ur-Ig-alim (`DRM-011`). That
does not show they spoke Sumerian at home; a Sumerian archive writes people down
under names its scribes could use. And the designation does not show they spoke
anything else, because *dumu me-luh-ha* may be a patronymic on a personal name
rather than a statement of origin at all (`TR-R-004`). The label and the name
point in different directions, and neither settles the other.

## A NOTE ON THE OBJECTS THEMSELVES

For every object here the record keeps ten things apart: where the stone formed,
who analysed it, where it was worked, in whose tradition, who exported it, who
carried it in between, what a text called it, where it was dug up, what a museum
wrote on its label, and who holds it now.

Filling that in honestly, for thirteen objects, **exporter and intermediary came
out empty for all thirteen**, and so did whether an openly licensed image
exists.

The last one is not a fact about antiquity. It is a fact about this session:
every museum database that would answer it is blocked (`DRM-023`, `D-055`). And
custody is stated as of **August 2022**, the most recent date in the catalogue
used — which for objects recorded in Baghdad, Idlib, Mosul and Kabul is not a
formality (`DRM-022`).

Several museums repeating one attribution is one source, not confirmation.
